THE POOLED ARITHMETIC THAT SPLITS SHARED GPU HOURS EVENLY

In this article ❧ Cloud teams rarely spend their compute grants in full. Leftover capacity from one squad can cover the shortfall of another, but only if the platform lead knows how a mixed pool divides into fair shares. This problem shows how a short chain of arithmetic turns uneven contributions into one equal allocation, and how a named-property proof makes each step defensible.

The Problem

A platform lead allotted 20 GPU hours to each squad for the sprint. One squad had already burned 5 of its 20 hours, so 15 remained. That squad joined a pod with a second squad and 3 squads that had received no allocation. The second squad still held all 20 of its hours.

If the pod pools every remaining GPU hour and splits them equally across its member squads, how many hours does each squad get?

  • a) 35
  • b) 11 and 2/3
  • c) 17 and 1/2
  • d) 7
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: d) 7 ✅

Math Solution

The pod holds five squads. Add the contributions, 15 from the first squad, 20 from the second, and 0 from each of the three empty squads, for a pooled total of 35 hours. Then divide 35 by 5 to reach the equal share.

Only two squads contribute, yet the pool still serves five, so the equal share sits well below any single allocation.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived, with the property that licenses each step:

  1. Additive inverse: 20 minus 5 equals 15, the first squad's remaining hours.
  2. Additive identity: 0 plus 0 plus 0 equals 0, the three empty squads contribute nothing.
  3. Addition laws: 15 plus 20 plus 0 equals 35, the pooled total.
  4. Substitution: the governing equation becomes 5s equals 35, where s is each squad's share.
  5. Multiplication property of equality: multiply each side by one over 5.
  6. Multiplicative inverse and identity: the left side collapses to s.
  7. Cancellation law: 35 over 5 equals 7 on the right side.
  8. Transitivity of equality: therefore s equals 7.

Historical Background

Splitting a shared stock into equal parts is one of the oldest recorded uses of arithmetic. Grain, land, and inheritance were divided among claimants long before algebra existed, and clerks in Mesopotamia and Egypt kept ledgers for exactly this purpose. The same logic now governs how cloud platforms ration compute, where a pool of unused capacity is redistributed so no team is starved and none hoards.
