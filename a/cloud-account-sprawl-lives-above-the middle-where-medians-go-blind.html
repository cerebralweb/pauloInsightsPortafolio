CLOUD ACCOUNT SPRAWL LIVES ABOVE THE MIDDLE, WHERE MEDIANS GO BLIND

In this article ❧ A single summary statistic is often the only figure that survives the trip from a survey to a slide, and teams then build plans on whatever it seems to imply. Knowing which claims a statistic licenses, and which it merely permits, is the difference between a defensible consolidation target and a guess. This problem shows how a positional measure fixes the middle of a distribution while leaving its total and its upper end entirely undetermined, and how a few lines of Python turn that reading into a repeatable check.

The Problem

Carolina surveyed the engineering teams in her division to find out how many paid cloud accounts each team manages. Using a box plot, she found that the median was 2.

Which of the following statements cannot be confirmed with the information provided?

  • a) At least half of the teams manage 2 or more accounts
  • b) At least one quarter of the teams manage 2 or more accounts
  • c) Determining whether teams manage 2 accounts on average requires additional data
  • d) The teams that manage the most accounts manage 2
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: d) The teams that manage the most accounts manage 2 ✅

Math Solution

With teams ranked by account count, the median sits at position (n+1)/2, so half the teams fall at or below it and half at or above it. That fixes the middle and nothing else: the largest value sits at position n, which the median never locates.

A median of 2 is compatible with a top team running 2 accounts or 400, which is exactly why statement d cannot be confirmed rather than being simply false.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Definition of the median: with n teams ranked by account count, the value at position (n+1)/2 splits the group into two halves of equal size
  2. Test choice a: a median of 2 puts at least 0.5n teams at 2 accounts or more, so the claim holds
  3. Test choice b: 0.5n exceeds 0.25n, so a claim about at least a quarter follows from the claim about at least a half
  4. Test choice c: the mean requires every account count, since one team with 40 accounts moves it while the median stays at 2, so additional data is required
  5. Test choice d: the median marks position (n+1)/2, not position n, so the maximum is unconstrained and the claim cannot be confirmed
  6. Conclusion: statement d is the one Carolina cannot support from a median alone

Historical Background

Before it had a name. The shape predates the label. Box plots appeared in climatology and geography from at least 1933 under the name dispersion diagram, and A. L. Bowley was reportedly drawing them in lectures around 1897. Kenneth Haemer coined the term range bar in 1948 for a simple bar running from minimum to maximum. The range bar method appeared in print in Mary Eleanor Spear's Charting Statistics in 1952 and again in her Practical Charting Techniques in 1969. Page 166 of the 1952 book carries a range bar diagram with an interquartile box, which Tufte and Wickham have both credited as the first formulation of the box plot. Spear worked as a graphic analyst for United States federal agencies from the 1920s to the 1960s, including the Internal Revenue Service and the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and taught graphic representation of statistics at American University.

Tukey names it and sells it. Tukey introduced the box and whiskers plot as part of his exploratory data analysis toolkit in 1970, though it stayed obscure until the formal 1977 publication. Cox notes that Tukey supplied the name and that he, his colleagues and his students promoted the display energetically through the 1970s, in Tukey 1972, Velleman and Hoaglin 1981, and Hoaglin, Mosteller and Tukey 1983. Tukey credited neither Spear nor anyone else, and was widely recorded at the time as the inventor. The design choice that mattered was economy. The summaries are robust, sit on actual data points, need no tuning parameter, and were computable by hand.

The refinements. McGill, Tukey and Larsen published three variants in The American Statistician in 1978. The first encodes group size, the second signals rough significance of differences between medians, the third combines both. Box width varies with the number of points in the variable width version, and the notched version draws confidence intervals around the medians. Then came the audit. Frigge, Hoaglin and Iglewicz surveyed popular software packages in 1989 and found several competing definitions, produced by different choices of quartile formula and of the fences that mark an observation as outside, and they proposed a single standard form. Because each package used a different formula, each flagged different observations as outliers.

Where it stands. Wickham and Stryjewski surveyed four decades of extensions in 2011 and observe that the box plot is now one of the most used statistical graphics and one of the few chart types invented in the twentieth century to reach general adoption. The original constraint was pencil and paper. With a computer on every desk that constraint relaxed, which opened the way to variants carrying density information, such as the violin plot, and to two dimensional versions. Those variants exist to fix known failures of the original, notably its blindness to multiple peaks and its habit of flagging too many outliers when the sample is large. The scenario labelled Two camps above is exactly the first failure.

Python Version

The script collects one account count per team, then reports the median together with the two figures the median cannot supply, so the same reading works for any survey of counts.

# What this does: finds the middle number in a list of team account counts.
# Where it came from: a manager surveyed her teams on how many paid cloud accounts each one runs.
# The math idea: sort the numbers, then take the one in the middle.
# You type in: how many teams answered, then each team's account count.
# It prints: the middle count, how many teams sit at or above it, and the highest count.

# This function finds the middle number.
def middle_count(counts):
    # Put the numbers in order from smallest to largest.
    ordered = sorted(counts)
    # Count how many numbers there are.
    how_many = len(ordered)
    # Find the spot in the middle.
    middle_spot = how_many // 2
    # If the count is odd, one number sits alone in the middle.
    if how_many % 2 == 1:
        return ordered[middle_spot]
    # If the count is even, average the two middle numbers.
    return (ordered[middle_spot - 1] + ordered[middle_spot]) / 2

# Tell the reader what we will figure out.
print("Let's find the middle number of cloud accounts per team.")

# Ask how many teams answered the survey.
team_total = int(input("How many teams answered: "))

# Start with an empty list to hold the answers.
account_counts = []

# Ask each team for its account count and add it to the list.
for team in range(team_total):
    one_count = int(input("Accounts for team " + str(team + 1) + ": "))
    account_counts.append(one_count)

# Find the middle number.
median = middle_count(account_counts)

# Show it as a whole number when it is one.
median = int(median) if float(median).is_integer() else median

# Count how many teams sit at the middle number or above it.
at_or_above = 0
for one_count in account_counts:
    if one_count >= median:
        at_or_above = at_or_above + 1

# Find the highest count, which the middle number does not tell you.
highest = max(account_counts)

# Print the answers with words that fit the scenario.
print("Middle number of paid cloud accounts per team:", median)
print("Teams at or above that number:", at_or_above, "out of", team_total)
print("Highest number of accounts run by one team:", highest)

The standard library version below is shorter and removes the risk of mishandling the even case, since statistics.median does the sorting and the split.

# What this does: finds the middle number in a list of team account counts.
# Where it came from: a manager surveyed her teams on how many paid cloud accounts each one runs.
# The math idea: sort the numbers, then take the one in the middle.
# You type in: how many teams answered, then each team's account count.
# It prints: the middle count, how many teams sit at or above it, and the highest count.

# Bring in Python's own tools for averages and middles.
import statistics

# Tell the reader what we will figure out.
print("Let's find the middle number of cloud accounts per team.")

# Ask how many teams answered the survey.
team_total = int(input("How many teams answered: "))

# Start with an empty list to hold the answers.
account_counts = []

# Ask each team for its account count and add it to the list.
for team in range(team_total):
    one_count = int(input("Accounts for team " + str(team + 1) + ": "))
    account_counts.append(one_count)

# Let Python find the middle number for us.
median = statistics.median(account_counts)

# Show it as a whole number when it is one.
median = int(median) if float(median).is_integer() else median

# Count how many teams sit at the middle number or above it.
at_or_above = sum(1 for one_count in account_counts if one_count >= median)

# Find the highest count, which the middle number does not tell you.
highest = max(account_counts)

# Print the answers with words that fit the scenario.
print("Middle number of paid cloud accounts per team:", median)
print("Teams at or above that number:", at_or_above, "out of", team_total)
print("Highest number of accounts run by one team:", highest)

# Team Count Median Check

Takes a set of whole number counts reported one per team, returns the median, the number of teams at or above it, and the highest count. The last two figures exist to separate what the median confirms from what it leaves unknown.

---

## Dependencies

None beyond standard tooling.

---

## Inputs

- **Number of teams**, a whole number, the count of responses collected.
- **Count per team**, one whole number for each team, e.g. paid cloud accounts managed.

---

## Steps

### Step 1 Collect the counts

Ask how many teams responded. Ask for each team's count in turn, one at a time, labelling each prompt with the team number. Store them as a single list. Reject anything that is not a whole number.

### Step 2 Find the median

Sort the counts from smallest to largest. If the number of teams is odd, take the single middle value. If it is even, average the two middle values. Report a whole number result without a decimal point.

### Step 3 Count the teams at or above the median

Count how many teams reported a value equal to or greater than the median. Report it against the total.

### Step 4 Find the highest count

Report the largest value in the list.

### Step 5 Return the reading

Print three labelled lines in this order, the median, the teams at or above it, the highest count. State in one line that the median fixes the middle of the distribution and confirms nothing about the mean or the maximum.

---

## Example Trigger Prompts

- "Each team told me how many cloud accounts they run. What is the middle number?"
- "I have a box plot with a median of 2. What can I actually claim from it?"
- "Find the median of these counts and tell me how many are above it."
- "Does a median of 2 mean the biggest team runs 2?"

---

The skill is worth keeping because the reading generalises. Any survey that collects one count per respondent produces the same three figures and the same warning about the upper end.
