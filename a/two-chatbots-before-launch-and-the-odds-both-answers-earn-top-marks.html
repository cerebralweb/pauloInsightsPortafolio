TWO CHATBOTS BEFORE LAUNCH, AND THE ODDS BOTH ANSWERS EARN TOP MARKS

In this article ❧ AI teams review samples of chatbot output before a launch. A single sample tells little, so teams draw from more than one system and ask how often the draws agree on quality. This problem shows how the chances of two separate draws combine into one figure, and how Python turns that step into a reusable tool.

The Problem

An AI team tests two customer support chatbots before launch. Reviewers grade every response from Chatbot A. Half receive a Superior rating and half receive a Basic rating. For Chatbot B, reviewers report 25% Superior, 25% High, 25% Basic, and 25% Low.

The product manager asks you to pick 1 response at random from each chatbot. What is the probability that both responses received a Superior rating?

  • a) 12.5%
  • b) 37.5%
  • c) 50%
  • d) 25%
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: a) 12.5% ✅

Math Solution

The two draws do not affect each other, so the events are independent. The chance that both occur is the product of the two chances, (1/2)(1/4) = 1/8.

One draw in 8 returns two Superior responses, which equals 12.5%.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Chatbot A share: 50% = 50/100 = 1/2
  2. Chatbot B share: 25% = 25/100 = 1/4
  3. Independent draws: P(A and B) = P(A)P(B)
  4. Product: (1/2)(1/4) = 1/8
  5. As a percent: 1/8 = 12.5/100 = 12.5%

Proof

Let A be the event that the response drawn from Chatbot A has a Superior rating, and B the event that the response drawn from Chatbot B has a Superior rating. The two draws are separate, so A and B are independent.

Notation

  • P(E) is the probability of an event E, a number from 0 to 1.
  • P(A) is the probability of a Superior response from Chatbot A, 50%.
  • P(B) is the probability of a Superior response from Chatbot B, 25%.
  • A ∩ B is the event that A and B both occur.
  • P(A ∩ B) is the probability that both responses are Superior, the unknown.
  • % reads per hundred.
  • E and F are any two events, used to state a property in general form.
  • x, y, m, n, c are any real numbers, used to state a property in general form.
  • ⇒ reads implies.

Step 1 — Definition of percent

Property. Definition of percent. x% = x/100.

Inference. P(A) = 50% = 50/100.

Step 2 — Cancellation law

Property. Cancellation law. For y ≠ 0, (yx)/(ym) = x/m.

Inference. 50/100 = (50)(1)/((50)(2)) = 1/2. From line 1.

Step 3 — Definition of percent, again

Property. Definition of percent, as in Step 1.

Inference. P(B) = 25% = 25/100.

Step 4 — Cancellation law, again

Property. Cancellation law, as in Step 2.

Inference. 25/100 = (25)(1)/((25)(4)) = 1/4. From line 3.

Step 5 — Multiplication rule for independent events

Property. Multiplication rule for independent events. E and F independent ⇒ P(E ∩ F) = P(E)P(F).

Inference. P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B) = (1/2)(1/4). From lines 2 and 4.

Step 6 — Product of fractions

Property. Product of fractions. (x/m)(y/n) = (xy)/(mn), for m ≠ 0 and n ≠ 0.

Inference. (1/2)(1/4) = (1)(1)/((2)(4)) = 1/8. From line 5.

Step 7 — Multiplicative identity

Property. Multiplicative identity. For c ≠ 0, c/c = 1 and (1)x = x.

Inference. 1/8 = (12.5/12.5)(1/8) = 12.5/100. From line 6.

Step 8 — Definition of percent, applied in reverse

Property. Definition of percent, as in Step 1.

Inference. 12.5/100 = 12.5%, so P(A ∩ B) = 12.5%. From line 7.

Verification

(1/2)(1/4) = 1/8, and (8)(12.5) = 100, so 1/8 = 12.5/100 = 12.5%. Both sides match.

Historical Background

The rule for combining separate chances took shape in the 1654 letters between Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat, who studied how to split the stakes of an unfinished game. Abraham de Moivre gave independence its first formal statement in The Doctrine of Chances in 1718. Insurers, quality inspectors, and today AI evaluation teams use the same rule when they sample from more than one source.

Python Version

The script asks for the Superior share of each chatbot and returns the chance that both picks are Superior, so it works for any pair of shares.

# What this does. Works out the chance that two picked responses are both rated Superior.
# Where it came from. A team tested two chatbots and picked one response from each at random.
# The math idea. When two picks do not affect each other, multiply their chances.
# You type in. The percent of Superior responses for each chatbot.
# It prints. The chance, in percent, that both picked responses are Superior.

# This function does the math.
def both_superior(share_a_percent, share_b_percent):
    # Turn each percent into a part of one. So 50 becomes 0.5.
    part_a = share_a_percent / 100
    part_b = share_b_percent / 100
    # Multiply the two chances, then turn the result back into a percent.
    return part_a * part_b * 100

# Tell the reader what we will figure out.
print("What is the chance that both picked chatbot responses are rated Superior?")

# Ask for the Superior share of Chatbot A.
share_a_percent = float(input("Percent of Chatbot A responses rated Superior? "))

# Ask for the Superior share of Chatbot B.
share_b_percent = float(input("Percent of Chatbot B responses rated Superior? "))

# Do the math.
result = both_superior(share_a_percent, share_b_percent)

# Show a whole number when there is no decimal part.
result = int(result) if result.is_integer() else result

# Print the answer with words that fit the scenario.
print("Chance that both responses are rated Superior", str(result) + "%")

Paste the code into Python in Windows Terminal, type 50 and 25, and the script returns 12.5%.
