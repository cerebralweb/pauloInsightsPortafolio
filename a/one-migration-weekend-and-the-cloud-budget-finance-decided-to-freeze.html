ONE MIGRATION WEEKEND, AND THE CLOUD BUDGET FINANCE DECIDED TO FREEZE

In this article ❧ Cloud spend rarely arrives as one flat line. A year of routine operations runs alongside a handful of one off events, and finance has to know which of the two moved the total before it can set a limit for the next cycle. This problem shows how a share of a total separates steady operations from an isolated event, and how each step of that reasoning rests on a named property of arithmetic.

The Problem

A startup spent 168,000 USD on cloud services during the fiscal year. Routine operations took 147,000 USD and the rest went to one data migration weekend, after which finance froze the budget.

What share of the yearly spend went to the migration weekend?

  • a) 1.25
  • b) 12.5 %
  • c) 12.5
  • d) 21 %
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: b) 12.5 % ✅

Math Solution

The two parts of the spend cover the whole year, so the migration figure is what remains once routine operations are subtracted from the total. That remainder is then divided by the total and scaled by 100, which gives 12.5 %.

The ratio 21000/168000 reduces to 1/8, so one eighth of the year's cloud spend sat in a single weekend.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Notation: T is the yearly spend, 168000. R is the routine operations spend, 147000. M is the migration weekend spend. p is the share as a percentage, the unknown.
  2. Subtraction property of equality: T = R + M ⇒ T − R = R + M − R
  3. Additive inverse and additive identity: R + M − R = M + 0 = M, so M = T − R
  4. Substitution: M = 168000 − 147000 = 21000
  5. Definition of a percentage: p = 100(M/T) = 100(21000/168000)
  6. Cancellation law: 21000/168000 = (21000)(1)/(21000)(8) = 1/8
  7. Transitivity of equality: p = 100(1/8) = 12.5
  8. Verification: 147000 + (12.5/100)(168000) = 147000 + 21000 = 168000 = T
  9. Answer: p = 12.5, so the migration weekend took 12.5 % of the yearly cloud spend

The Proof, Property by Property

The breakdown above gives the arithmetic. This section gives the licence for each move, one named property per step.

Notation

  • T is the yearly cloud spend in dollars, 168000.
  • R is the routine operations spend in dollars, 147000.
  • M is the migration weekend spend in dollars, unknown until Step 3.
  • p is the migration share expressed as a percentage, the unknown.
  • 100 is the scaling factor that turns a ratio into a percentage.
  • 1/8 is the reduced form of M/T, produced in Step 5.
  • ⇒ reads implies.
  • x, y, c are any real numbers, used to state a property in general form.

Step 1 — Subtraction property of equality

Property. Subtraction property of equality. x = y ⇒ x − c = y − c.

Inference. T = R + M ⇒ T − R = R + M − R.

Step 2 — Additive inverse and additive identity

Property. Additive inverse and additive identity. y + (−y) = 0 and x + 0 = x.

Inference. R + M − R = M + 0 = M, so M = T − R. From line 1.

Step 3 — Substitution

Property. Substitution. x = y ⇒ x may replace y.

Inference. M = 168000 − 147000 = 21000. From line 2.

Step 4 — Definition of a percentage

Property. Percentage. x as a percentage of y, y ≠ 0, is 100(x/y).

Inference. p = 100(21000/168000). From line 3.

Step 5 — Cancellation law

Property. Cancellation law. For y ≠ 0, (yx)/y = x.

Inference. 21000/168000 = (21000)(1)/(21000)(8) = 1/8. From line 4.

Step 6 — Transitivity of equality

Property. Transitivity of equality. x = y and y = z ⇒ x = z.

Inference. p = 100(1/8) = 12.5. From lines 4 and 5.

Verification

147000 + (12.5/100)(168000) = 147000 + 21000 = 168000 = T. Both sides match.

Answer

p = 12.5. The migration weekend took 12.5 % of the yearly cloud spend, which is option b.

Historical Background

Reckoning in hundredths entered administration through taxation. Rome levied the centesima rerum venalium, a levy of one part in a hundred on goods sold at auction, which trained clerks to think of a payment as a fixed share of a total rather than a flat sum. Merchant banking in Florence and Venice carried the habit into ledgers, where profit, commission, and loss were all recorded as parts of a hundred so that deals of different sizes could be compared on one scale. Cloud finance teams inherit the same device when they ask how much of a yearly bill one event absorbed.
