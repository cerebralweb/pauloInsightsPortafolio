56 CLEARED BATCHES, AND WHAT THE RETRAINING WINDOW CAN HOLD

In this article ❧ Machine learning teams commit to retraining dates before they know whether the data will be ready. The commitment rests on human throughput, on how much review work one person clears in the hours a calendar week allows, and a manager who guesses that figure ships a model on stale labels. This problem shows how one observed stretch of work fixes a rate, how that rate scales to any block of time, and how the direction of the rate decides whether the answer is right or off by half.

The Problem

An annotation specialist validates labeled image batches before a computer vision model is retrained. Every 5 hours of review, she clears 7 batches. Her manager asks for as many cleared batches as possible in one week, with 40 hours of work available, because the retraining window opens the following Monday.

How many batches can she clear?

  • a) 56
  • b) 28
  • c) 29
  • d) 28.40
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: a) 56 ✅

Math Solution

The observed stretch gives the ratio of 7 batches to 5 hours. Divide to get 1.4 batches per hour, then multiply by the 40 hours available to reach 56 batches. The same result arrives by counting blocks, since 40 hours holds 8 stretches of 5 hours, and 8 times 7 is 56.

The constant carries units, 1.4 batches per hour, and those units are what catch a reversed rate before it reaches the answer sheet.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Observed stretch: 7 batches in 5 hours, written as the ratio 7 over 5
  2. Constant of proportionality: 7 divided by 5 gives 1.4 batches per hour
  3. Scale to the week: 1.4 times 40 gives 56 batches
  4. Check by blocks: 40 divided by 5 gives 8 stretches, and 8 times 7 gives 56
  5. Distractor check: the values near 28 come from dividing 40 by 1.4, which answers how many hours a stated output would take and inverts the rate
  6. Result: 56 batches cleared in the 40 hour week, option a

The Proof, Step by Step

The breakdown above shows the arithmetic. This section shows the licence for each move, since a step that gets the right number by the wrong warrant will fail on the next problem.

Setup

Let A be the batches cleared and H the hours worked. Output is proportional to hours, so A over H holds one fixed value. The observed stretch fixes that value at 7 batches over 5 hours. The week fixes H at 40. So A over 40 equals 7 over 5.

Notation

  • A is the batches cleared in the week, the unknown, a nonnegative rational
  • H is the hours worked, 40 in this run
  • k is the constant of proportionality in batches per hour, 7 over 5 once Setup fixes it
  • 7 is the batches cleared in the observed stretch, and 5 is the hours that stretch took
  • 40 is the hours available in the week

Solution

  1. Substitution. Equals may replace equals. The governing equation is A over 40 equals 7 over 5, replacing H with 40 in A over H equals k, and k with 7 over 5.
  2. Multiplication property of equality. If a equals b then c times a equals c times b. So 40 times the quantity A over 40 equals 40 times 7 over 5. Builds on line 1. The same factor multiplies each side.
  3. Cancellation law. For nonzero b, b times a over b equals a. The left side equals A. Builds on line 2. The 40 cancels.
  4. Associativity of multiplication. The grouping of factors does not change the product. The right side equals 280 times one over 5. Builds on line 2, regrouping 40 times 7 times one over 5 as the quantity 40 times 7, then one over 5.
  5. Cancellation law, as in line 3. The right side equals 56. Builds on line 4, since 280 over 5 is 5 times 56 over 5, and the 5 cancels.
  6. Transitivity of equality. If a equals b and b equals c then a equals c. Therefore A equals 56. Builds on lines 3 and 5.

Verification

Substitute 56 into the constraint. 56 over 40 equals 7 over 5, since 56 is 8 times 7 and 40 is 8 times 5. Both sides match.

A constant of proportionality belongs to a direction, so 7 over 5 answers for batches and 5 over 7 answers for hours, and the two are reciprocals of one relationship.

Historical Background

Output per hour became a management figure in the early twentieth century. Frederick Winslow Taylor timed individual work operations with a stopwatch and published the method in The Principles of Scientific Management in 1911, converting each observed stretch of labor into a rate that planners could scale to a shift, a week, or a season.

Frank and Lillian Gilbreth extended the approach through motion study, breaking tasks into components and timing each one. Their per hour figures spread from factory floors into wage schedules, production quotas, and delivery commitments. The same figure now sets service level targets in software operations, where a review rate decides whether a release date holds.

Python Version

The script turns the observed stretch into a rate and scales it to any block of hours, so it answers the same question for a different specialist, a different pace, or a shorter week.

# What this does: works out how many batches get cleared in a whole week.
# Where it came from: an annotation specialist cleared 7 batches every 5 hours,
#   and her manager wanted the total for a 40 hour week.
# The math idea: find how much gets done in one hour, then stretch it to all the hours.
# You type in: the batches cleared, the hours that took, and the hours in the week.
# It prints: how many batches get cleared in the week.

# This function does the math.
def batches_in_week(batches_seen, hours_seen, hours_available):
    # Work out how many batches get done in a single hour.
    per_hour = batches_seen / hours_seen
    # Stretch that one hour amount across all the hours available.
    return per_hour * hours_available

# Tell the reader what we will figure out.
print("Let's find how many batches get cleared in the week.")

# Ask how many batches were cleared in the stretch we watched.
batches_seen = float(input("Batches cleared in the stretch: "))

# Ask how many hours that stretch took.
hours_seen = float(input("Hours that stretch took: "))

# Ask how many hours are available in the week.
hours_available = float(input("Hours available this week: "))

# Stop if the hours watched is zero, because we cannot divide by zero.
if hours_seen == 0:
    print("The hours of the stretch cannot be zero.")
else:
    # Do the math.
    result = batches_in_week(batches_seen, hours_seen, hours_available)

    # Show a whole number when the answer comes out even.
    result = int(result) if result.is_integer() else round(result, 2)

    # Print the answer with words that fit the scenario.
    print("Batches cleared in the week:", result)

Run it, type 7, then 5, then 40, and it returns 56 batches for the week.
