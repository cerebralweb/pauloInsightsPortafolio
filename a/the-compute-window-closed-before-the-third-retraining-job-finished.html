THE COMPUTE WINDOW CLOSED BEFORE THE THIRD RETRAINING JOB FINISHED

In this article ❧ Capacity planning for machine learning teams rests on two fixed quantities, the throughput of the pipeline and the length of the window the schedule allows. Partial output carries no value in that setting, since a model that stops mid training ships nothing, so the planner needs the count of finished units rather than the total volume of work. This problem shows how a fractional rate applied to a fractional window yields a total that must then be cut down to whole deliverables, and how a short Python function turns that logic into a reusable planning tool.

The Problem

Melisa manages an MLOps pipeline before a product launch. The pipeline completes 2/5 of a model retraining job in one hour. She has 5 and 1/4 hours of compute window before the launch.

How many retraining jobs can the pipeline complete?

  • a) 4
  • b) 3 and a half
  • c) 4 and 3/4
  • d) 3
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: none of the listed options. The pipeline finishes 2 jobs ✅

Math Solution

The window converts to 21/4 hours, and multiplying the rate 2/5 by 21/4 gives 42/20, which reduces to 21/10, or 2.1 jobs. Taking the floor of 2.1 leaves 2 finished jobs and one tenth of a job stranded.

The extra quarter hour buys 0.1 of a job, so a 5 and 1/4 hour window delivers exactly what a 5 hour window delivers.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Rate: 2/5 of a job per hour, or 0.4 jobs per hour
  2. Window as an improper fraction: 5 and 1/4 becomes 21/4, or 5.25 hours
  3. Total output: 2/5 times 21/4 equals 42/20
  4. Reduced total: 42/20 equals 21/10, or 2.1 jobs
  5. Whole jobs: the floor of 2.1 equals 2
  6. Residual: 2.1 minus 2 equals 0.1 of a job, below the 1 needed to deliver
  7. Answer: 2 finished retraining jobs, with a partial third abandoned

Justified Solution

The breakdown above shows the arithmetic. This section names the property that licenses each move, so no step rests on habit.

Mode. Compute.

Setup. Let r be the pipeline rate, t the compute window, N the total output, and J the count of finished jobs. The pipeline fixes r at 2/5 of a job per hour. The launch schedule fixes t at 5 and 1/4 hours. Output is r times t, so N equals 2/5 times 5 and 1/4, and J is the whole part of N.

Notation.

  • r, the pipeline rate in jobs per hour, 2/5
  • t, the compute window in hours, 5 and 1/4
  • N, the total output in jobs, the unknown of the multiplication
  • J, the count of finished jobs, the unknown the question asks for, a nonnegative integer

Step 1. Definition of a mixed number

Property. A mixed number equals the sum of its integer part and its fractional part.

Inference. t equals 21/4. Five plus 1/4 is 20/4 plus 1/4, which is 21/4.

Step 2. Product rule for fractions

Property. The product of a over b and c over d equals a times c over b times d.

Inference. N equals 42/20. Builds on line 1. Two times 21 is 42, and five times four is 20.

Step 3. Cancellation law

Property. For nonzero b, b times a over b equals a.

Inference. N equals 21/10. Builds on line 2. 42/20 is 2 times 21 over 2 times 10, and the 2 cancels.

Step 4. Division algorithm

Property. For integers a and b with b positive, there are unique integers q and s with a equal to b times q plus s, where s is at least zero and less than b.

Inference. 21 equals 10 times 2 plus 1, so N equals 2 plus 1/10. Builds on line 3. The quotient is 2 and the remainder is 1, which sits below the divisor 10.

Step 5. Definition of the floor function

Property. The floor of a number is the greatest integer not exceeding it.

Inference. J equals 2. Builds on line 4. N sits at 2 plus 1/10, so N is at least 2 and below 3, which puts the greatest integer not exceeding N at 2.

Verification

Substituting r and t back into the constraint gives 0.4 times 5.25, which is 2.1, matching 21/10 from line 3. The bound from line 5 holds, since 2 is at most 2.1 and 2.1 is below 3.

Answer

The pipeline finishes 2 retraining jobs. Total output reaches 2.1 jobs, so one tenth of a third job stays unfinished when the window closes.

Historical Background

Work rate problems entered commercial arithmetic through the trades, where a master needed to know how much of a job an apprentice could finish in a day and how many days a contract allowed. Medieval Italian abacus schools trained merchants on exactly this pairing, a fractional rate against a fractional stretch of time, because payment often turned on completed pieces rather than hours logged.

The cutting step has its own commercial history. Simon Stevin argued in 1585 that decimal notation should replace common fractions for merchants and mint officials, since a figure such as 2.1 reads faster than 21/10 when a clerk must decide how many finished units to record. The distinction between the volume of work performed and the number of deliverables accepted survives in modern billing, manufacturing yield, and cloud capacity planning.

Python Version

The script separates total output from deliverable output, so any rate and any window can be tested without redoing the arithmetic by hand.

from fractions import Fraction
from math import floor


def jobs_completed(rate_per_hour, hours):
    """Return total output, finished jobs, and stranded work.

    rate_per_hour and hours accept ints, floats, or Fraction values.
    """
    rate = Fraction(rate_per_hour)
    window = Fraction(hours)
    total = rate * window
    finished = floor(total)
    stranded = total - finished
    return {
        "total_output": total,
        "finished_jobs": finished,
        "stranded_work": stranded,
    }


if __name__ == "__main__":
    result = jobs_completed(Fraction(2, 5), Fraction(21, 4))
    print("total output:", result["total_output"])
    print("finished jobs:", result["finished_jobs"])
    print("stranded work:", result["stranded_work"])

Run it with python jobs.py and it reports a total of 21/10, two finished jobs, and 1/10 of a job left unfinished.
