WHEN A SECOND ASSOCIATE JOINS THE BEVERAGE AISLE RESTOCK RUN

In this article ❧ Retail floor labor is planned in repetitions, not in totals. A store lead needs to know what a task costs per cycle, what changes when a second associate absorbs part of the load, and whether the work closes before the shift does. This problem shows how one signed quantity, the additive inverse, carries a staffing calculation from the shop floor to the axioms of algebra.

The Problem

Maria restocks the beverage aisle at a Walmart store. Each trip from the stockroom she carries 17 cases of soda. One trip will also need 3 cases of bottled water. With 6 trips left before the shift ends, Magda offers to carry 3 cases of soda on each trip and 1 of the water cases on the trip that includes them.

How many cases will Maria carry over the remaining trips?

  • a) 85
  • b) 86
  • c) 92
  • d) 102
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: b) 86 ✅

Math Solution

Magda's share reduces the load per trip from 17 to 14, so the recurring work is 6 trips at 14 cases. The water load is a one time addition, 3 reduced to 2, which brings the total to 86.

The trip is the unit of work, so the whole task prices out as a rate of 14 cases per trip plus a fixed 2.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Net soda per trip: 17 + (−3) = 14 cases
  2. Soda across the remaining trips: 6(14) = 84 cases
  3. Net water cases: 3 + (−1) = 2 cases
  4. Total: 84 + 2 = 86 cases
  5. Conclusion: Maria carries 86 cases over the 6 remaining trips

Why This Matters on the Floor

Maria needs one number before the shift ends, not a running tally. The calculation turns a repeated load into a rate per trip, then applies that rate to the trips left. Splitting the work with Magda changes the rate, not the structure, so the same expression answers any variation of the question.

Store leads use the same move to size labor, to test whether a second associate closes the gap before the shift ends, and to plan how many trips a task will take. The general form separates the two parts of any such task, a recurring load and a fixed load, which is what makes it reusable across aisles, shifts, and stores.

Mathematical Background

The problem rests on signed addition, the commutative and associative laws, and the distributive law. Four routes reach the same result.

Written out in full, with every trip listed.

3 + (−1) + 17 + 17 + 17 + 17 + 17 + 17 + (−3) + (−3) + (−3) + (−3) + (−3) + (−3) = 86

Regrouped, which is the commutative law at work, since reordering the terms cannot change the sum.

2 + 102 + (−18) = 86

Factored, which is the distributive law, since six identical net loads collapse into one product.

3 + (−1) + 6(17 + (−3)) = 86

The fastest route for mental arithmetic leads with the multiplication, then pairs the additions against the subtractions.

102 + 3 + (−18) + (−1) = 105 + (−19) = 86

Generalized, with s for trips remaining.

(17s + 3) − (3s + 1) = 17s + 3 + (−3s) + (−1) = 14s + 2

At s = 6, 14(6) + 2 = 86. The form 14s + 2 is the payoff. It prices any number of trips without redoing the arithmetic, and it names the two parts of the work, a recurring load of 14 and a fixed load of 2.

Step by Step, With the Property Named

Setup

Maria makes a fixed number of trips, carrying the same soda load on each one, plus a water load on a single trip. Magda removes a fixed share of the soda on every trip and part of the water load once. Let s be the trips remaining and L the cases Maria carries. Her gross load is 17s + 3. Magda's share is 3s + 1. So L = (17s + 3) − (3s + 1), with s = 6.

Notation

  • s is the trips remaining, 6.
  • L is the cases Maria carries over those trips, the unknown, a positive integer.
  • 17 is the soda cases loaded per trip before Magda's help, and 3s is Magda's soda share across the trips.
  • 3 is the water cases on the single water trip, and 1 is the water case Magda takes.
  • −1 is the additive inverse of 1, and −3s is the additive inverse of 3s.
  • 0 is the additive identity.
  • x, y, c are any integers, used to state a property in general form.
  • ⇒ reads implies.

Solution

  1. Subtraction as addition of the additive inverse. Property, x − y = x + (−1)y. Inference, L = (17s + 3) + (−1)(3s + 1).
  2. Distributivity of multiplication over addition. Property, c(x + y) = cx + cy. Inference, (−1)(3s + 1) = (−3s) + (−1), so L = 17s + 3 + (−3s) + (−1). From line 1.
  3. Commutative and associative laws of addition. Property, x + y = y + x and (x + y) + z = x + (y + z). Inference, L = [17s + (−3s)] + [3 + (−1)]. From line 2.
  4. Distributivity, read right to left. Property, cx + cy = c(x + y). Inference, 17s + (−3s) = [17 + (−3)]s = 14s. From line 3.
  5. Additive inverse and additive identity. Property, x + (−x) = 0 and x + 0 = x. Inference, 3 + (−1) = 2 + [1 + (−1)] = 2 + 0 = 2. From line 3.
  6. Substitution. Property, x = y ⇒ x may replace y. Inference, L = 14s + 2 and s = 6, so L = 14(6) + 2. From lines 4 and 5.
  7. Distributivity, on the product. Property, as in line 2. Inference, 14(6) = (10 + 4)(6) = 60 + 24 = 84. From line 6.
  8. Transitivity of equality. Property, x = y and y = z ⇒ x = z. Inference, L = 84 + 2 = 86. From lines 6 and 7.

Verification

(17(6) + 3) − (3(6) + 1) = (102 + 3) − (18 + 1) = 105 − 19 = 86. Both sides match.

What the Additive Inverse Actually Does

The calculation above uses the additive inverse twice, once to erase a term and once to convert subtraction into addition. In algebra it does more. Every item below reduces to the first.

  1. Produces zero. x + (−x) = 0. This is the axiom. Everything else follows.
  2. Erases a term. x + a + (−a) = x + 0 = x. Paired with the additive identity, an unwanted term disappears. This is line 5 of the solution above.
  3. Defines subtraction. x − y = x + (−y). Subtraction is not a separate operation, which is why the commutative and associative laws reach subtraction expressions once they are rewritten.
  4. Solves x + a = b uniquely. x = b + (−a). Existence comes from the inverse, uniqueness from cancellation, x + a = y + a ⇒ x = y. In practice this is the addition property of equality, moving a term across the equals sign.
  5. Distributes as multiplication by −1. (−1)y = −y, and −(x + y) = (−x) + (−y). Sign changes propagate through sums and products by the distributive law, not by a separate rule.
  6. Closes the naturals into the integers. Adjoining inverses to the natural numbers gives the integers. The same step turns a semiring into a ring.
  7. Makes addition a group. Closure, associativity, identity, and inverses together. Group structure is what guarantees items 2 and 4 and the cancellation law, rather than each being an independent fact.
  8. Reverses order. x < y ⇒ (−y) < (−x). In an ordered field the inverse reflects the order across 0, which is why multiplying an inequality by a negative number flips it.
  9. Gives absolute value and distance. |x| is x or −x, whichever is not negative, and |x − y| is the distance between them. Metric ideas rest on the inverse.

Items 1 through 7 hold in any ring. Items 8 and 9 need an order.

Which Operation Is Basic

If subtraction is only notation for addition of the inverse, the reverse question follows. Could addition be notation for subtraction? It could. Subtraction can be taken as the undefined operation, and then x + y = x − (0 − y). The two are interdefinable, so the theory proves the same theorems either way.

What settles the convention is not truth but economy. A primitive is a term a theory leaves undefined and builds on, fixed only by the axioms it obeys. In the ring axioms, addition and multiplication are primitive. Addition earns that place because it is associative and commutative, and subtraction is neither. Axioms stated over addition are short. Stated over subtraction, every law would carry qualifications.

Historical Background

Signed quantities are older than the notation for them. The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, compiled in Han era China, handled positive and negative amounts with counting rods of two colors, a method used for surveying, grain accounting, and tax assessment. Liu Hui's third century commentary stated the rules for combining them.

Europe resisted negative results for another thousand years, treating a debt as a quantity to be spoken about rather than a number to be carried through a calculation. Merchant bookkeeping in Renaissance Italy forced the issue, since a ledger with credits and debits needs one arithmetic, not two. The logic that lets a store lead net a colleague's share out of a workload is the same logic that let a ledger close.
