THREE PROOFS, ONE REVENUE: HOW A SINGLE PART REBUILDS THE WHOLE

In this article ❧ A firm rarely knows its whole budget up front. It knows one measured line, the cost of a single project, while every other bucket is a share of an unknown total. Fractions let you name each share, chain a fraction of a fraction, and back out the whole from that one part. The same figure can be reached by more than one valid route, and comparing those routes shows which reasoning a reader can trust and repeat.

The Problem

A consulting firm splits its monthly revenue this way. Half covers operating costs. One fifth of the remaining half goes into a reserve fund. The rest splits evenly across 3 client projects. Each project costs 400,000.

What is the firm's monthly revenue?

  • a) 3,000,000
  • b) 3,200,000
  • c) 2,800,000
  • d) 2,900,000
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: a) 3,000,000 ✅

Math Solution

The projects take two fifths of revenue, which equals 3 × 400,000 = 1,200,000. That share fixes the whole. Two fifths is 40 percent, so 10 percent = 300,000 and 100 percent = 3,000,000.

One known part, priced once, sets the value of every other part.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Ops: one half of revenue
  2. Fund: one fifth of the other half, so one tenth of revenue
  3. Projects: the rest of the half, four tenths, or two fifths
  4. Known part: 3 × 400,000 = 1,200,000 = two fifths of revenue
  5. Whole: 10 percent = 300,000, so revenue = 3,000,000

Three Proofs, Three Paths

Every proof below reaches 3,000,000. Each names the property that licenses each move and shows the equation. They differ in entry point and in how heavy the arithmetic gets.

Proof 1 — Chained fractions

Build the project fraction from the top down, then invert.

  1. Multiplication of fractions. The fund is one tenth of R.
    1/5 × 1/2 = 1/10
  2. Subtraction of fractions. Projects take four tenths of R.
    1/2 − 1/10 = 5/10 − 1/10 = 4/10 = 2/5
  3. Division of a fraction by an integer. Each project share is two fifteenths of R.
    (2/5) ÷ 3 = 2/15
  4. Substitution. The share equals its known value.
    (2/15)R = 400,000
  5. Multiplication property of equality. Multiply each side by 15/2.
    (15/2) × (2/15)R = (15/2) × 400,000
  6. Multiplicative inverse and identity. The left side collapses to R.
    (15/2) × (2/15) = 1, so R = (15/2) × 400,000
  7. Multiplication of a number by a fraction. The right side resolves.
    (15/2) × 400,000 = 6,000,000 / 2 = 3,000,000
  8. Transitivity of equality.
    R = 3,000,000

Proof 2 — From the known parts

Let H be the half that is not ops. Start from the 3 known shares.

  1. Distributive law over addition. The 3 shares total 1,200,000.
    400,000 + 400,000 + 400,000 = 3 × 400,000 = 1,200,000
  2. Additive partition of a unit. Projects are four fifths of H, since the fund takes one fifth.
    1 − 1/5 = 4/5
  3. Substitution. That four fifths carries the total above.
    (4/5)H = 1,200,000
  4. Multiplication property of equality. Multiply each side by 5/4.
    (5/4) × (4/5)H = (5/4) × 1,200,000
  5. Multiplicative inverse and identity. The left side collapses to H.
    (5/4) × (4/5) = 1, so H = (5/4) × 1,200,000
  6. Multiplication of a number by a fraction. The half is 1,500,000.
    (5/4) × 1,200,000 = 6,000,000 / 4 = 1,500,000
  7. Multiplication property of equality. The revenue is twice the half.
    2H = 2 × (1/2)R = R
  8. Substitution and transitivity of equality. Replace H with 1,500,000.
    R = 2 × 1,500,000 = 3,000,000

Proof 3 — Tenths unit value

Cast every share as tenths of R. Price one tenth, then take ten.

  1. Equivalent fractions. Ops is five tenths of R, the half re-expressed over ten.
    ops = 1/2 = 5/10
  2. Multiplication of fractions. The fund is one tenth of R.
    fund = 1/5 × 1/2 = 1/10
  3. Additive partition of a unit. Projects fill the leftover four tenths.
    projects = 10/10 − 5/10 − 1/10 = 4/10
  4. Multiplication. The 3 shares are worth 1,200,000.
    3 × 400,000 = 1,200,000
  5. Substitution. That value matches the four tenths from the partition step.
    4/10 of R = 1,200,000
  6. Division property of equality. One tenth of R is worth 300,000.
    one tenth = 1,200,000 ÷ 4 = 300,000
  7. Multiplication property of equality. The whole is ten tenths.
    R = 10 × 300,000 = 3,000,000

All three verify. Ops is 1,500,000, the fund is 300,000, projects are 1,200,000, and 1,200,000 ÷ 3 = 400,000 each.

Proof 3 Works Better Because It Prices One Unit and Counts

Proof 3 is the strongest for a reader. It runs in the fewest steps, 7 against 8, and it wins clearly on arithmetic weight and intuition. The reason is the unit. Proof 3 turns every share into tenths of the same whole, so the shares share one denominator. That removes the awkward fraction. Proof 1 must carry two fifteenths of R, a number with no mental image, and invert it. Proof 2 scales twice, first to the half then to the whole, so a slip in either stage breaks the answer. Proof 3 asks one question. What is one tenth worth. Once one tenth is 300,000, the whole is ten of them.

Proof 3 also binds to the known figure early. The 400,000 prices a part in step 3, and every later line is plain counting. Proof 1 holds the given back until substitution, so the reader waits to see why it matters.

Proof 2 is the better teaching bridge when the reader already trusts that 3 × 400,000 fills four fifths of a half, because it starts from concrete money. Proof 1 is the most general and the least friendly, useful when you want the single fraction two fifteenths stated in full. For a first read and for low error risk, Proof 3 is the one to show.

Historical Background

Splitting a whole into named shares and recovering the whole from one known share is among the oldest recorded uses of arithmetic. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, copied in Egypt around 1550 BCE, is built almost entirely on unit fractions such as one half, one fifth, and one tenth, the same pieces this problem uses. Egyptian scribes solved allocation and division problems by decomposing a quantity into these fractions, then pricing one part to reach the rest.

The pattern survived because business needs it. Rationing grain, dividing an estate, allocating a budget across cost buckets, all reduce to the same move. Once every part is a share of one whole, a single measured amount fixes the total. The consulting firm here does what a scribe did, only the shares now carry names like ops, reserve, and project.
