225 LUNCH ORDERS, THREE OPTIONS, ONE RATIO CHAIN

In this article ❧ Advance ordering forces a kitchen to commit resources before demand is known, so the split across menu options has to be inferred from a few fixed relationships and one total. When each option is tied to another by a ratio, the whole distribution collapses to a single unknown. This problem shows how ratio relationships anchored to a known total let a planner recover every quantity from one equation, and how Python turns that recovery into a reusable tool.

The Problem

At a school, students order lunch in advance so the kitchen staff knows what to prepare. On Monday, six times as many students chose hamburgers as salads. The number of students who chose lasagna was one third the number who chose hamburgers.

If 225 students ordered lunch, how many chose hamburgers, given that hamburgers, salads, and lasagna were the only three options?

  • a) 25
  • b) 150
  • c) 50
  • d) 100
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: b) 150 ✅

Math Solution

Write salads as the base unit. Hamburgers are 6 times that base and lasagna is 2 times it, since one third of six is two. The three parts sum to nine base units, and nine units equal 225, so one unit is 25 and hamburgers are 150.

Every quantity rides on one hidden unit, and the total fixes that unit at 25 students.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Base unit: salads = s
  2. Hamburgers in units: 6s
  3. Lasagna in units: one third of 6s = 2s
  4. Total in units: s + 6s + 2s = 9s
  5. Solve the unit: 9s = 225 gives s = 25
  6. Hamburgers: 6 × 25 = 150
  7. Check: 25 + 150 + 50 = 225

Proof With Named Properties

Compute mode. Let s be salads, h hamburgers, and l lasagna, each a nonnegative integer. Two relations hold, h = 6s and l = h/3, and the three options sum to 225, so s + h + l = 225. The target is h.

Step 1. Substitution

Property. Substitution. Equals may replace equals.

Inference. l = 6s / 3. Replacing h with 6s in l = h/3 gives this.

Step 2. Cancellation law

Property. Cancellation law. For nonzero b, b times a over b equals a.

Inference. l = 2s. Builds on line 1. 6s / 3 is 3 times 2s over 3, and the 3 cancels.

Step 3. Substitution

Property. Substitution. Equals may replace equals.

Inference. s + 6s + 2s = 225. Builds on line 2 and the setup, taking h as 6s and l as 2s.

Step 4. Distributive law

Property. Distributive law. a times c plus b times c equals the quantity a plus b times c.

Inference. 9s = 225. Builds on line 3. The coefficients 1, 6, and 2 sum to 9.

Step 5. Multiplication property of equality

Property. Multiplication property of equality. If a equals b then c times a equals c times b.

Inference. One over 9 times 9s equals one over 9 times 225. Builds on line 4. The same factor multiplies each side.

Step 6. Multiplicative inverse and identity

Property. Multiplicative inverse property and multiplicative identity property. A nonzero number times its inverse is one, and one times a equals a.

Inference. The left side equals s. Builds on line 5. One over 9 times 9 is one, and one times s is s.

Step 7. Cancellation law

Property. Cancellation law, as in Step 2.

Inference. The right side equals 25. 225 / 9 is 9 times 25 over 9, and the 9 cancels.

Step 8. Transitivity of equality

Property. Transitivity of equality. If a equals b and b equals c then a equals c.

Inference. s = 25. Builds on lines 6 and 7.

Step 9. Substitution

Property. Substitution. Equals may replace equals.

Inference. h = 6 times 25, which is 150. Builds on line 8 and h = 6s.

Verification. With s = 25, h = 150, and l = 50, the sum is 225. The total matches. Hamburgers are 150.

Historical Background

Splitting a known whole into parts fixed by ratio is one of the oldest jobs in applied arithmetic. Merchants in ancient Mesopotamia divided harvests and silver among partners by set proportions, and scribes recorded these shares on clay tablets long before algebra had symbols. The same reasoning later drove tax assessment, army provisioning, and inheritance law, where one total and a handful of ratios settled every claimant's portion.

Python Version

This script recovers all three counts from a total, the hamburger to salad ratio, and the lasagna fraction, so any similar demand split reuses it.

from fractions import Fraction

def lunch_counts(total, burger_to_salad, lasagna_fraction_of_burger):
    ratio = Fraction(burger_to_salad)
    frac = Fraction(lasagna_fraction_of_burger)
    units = 1 + ratio + ratio * frac
    salads = Fraction(total) / units
    hamburgers = ratio * salads
    lasagna = frac * hamburgers
    return int(salads), int(hamburgers), int(lasagna)

print(lunch_counts(225, 6, Fraction(1, 3)))

Run it with any total and ratios, and it returns the salad, hamburger, and lasagna counts as whole numbers, printing (25, 150, 50) for this case.
