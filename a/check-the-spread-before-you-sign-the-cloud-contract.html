CHECK THE SPREAD BEFORE YOU SIGN THE CLOUD CONTRACT

In this article ❧ Platform teams pick infrastructure on the strength of a single number, the average runtime reported after a handful of trial executions. That number can be identical across two candidate configurations while hiding a difference that matters at every deployment, because an average says where the center sits and nothing about how far the runs wander from it. This problem shows why an average needs a second measure standing beside it before anyone commits to a configuration, and how a short Python script can compute both.

The Problem

A platform team runs the same nightly data pipeline on two cloud instance configurations and records the runtime of five executions on each. Configuration A produced runtimes of 9, 10, 11, 14, and 16 minutes. Configuration B produced runtimes of 6, 8, 12, 15, and 19 minutes.

Both configurations have the same mean runtime. In which case does the mean describe BETTER the runtime the team should expect from a typical execution?

  • a) in both cases
  • b) in the second case
  • c) in the first case
  • d) in neither case
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: c) in the first case ✅

Math Solution

Both sets sum to 60 minutes and divide by five executions, so both means equal 12. With the means tied, the decision falls to the average distance from the mean, which is 2.4 minutes for Configuration A and 4 minutes for Configuration B.

Additionally,configuration A stays within 4 minutes of 12, while Configuration B reaches 7 minutes away from it.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Sum for Configuration A 9 plus 10 plus 11 plus 14 plus 16 equals 60 minutes
  2. Mean for Configuration A 60 divided by 5 equals 12 minutes
  3. Sum for Configuration B 6 plus 8 plus 12 plus 15 plus 19 equals 60 minutes
  4. Mean for Configuration B 60 divided by 5 equals 12 minutes, so the two means tie
  5. Median check 11 for A and 12 for B, so neither set carries the one sided gap that marks skew and the mean stands as the measure of center
  6. Distances for Configuration A 3, 2, 1, 2, 4, summing to 12 minutes
  7. Average distance for Configuration A 12 divided by 5 equals 2.4 minutes
  8. Distances for Configuration B 6, 4, 0, 3, 7, summing to 20 minutes
  9. Average distance for Configuration B 20 divided by 5 equals 4 minutes
  10. Comparison 2.4 is less than 4, so Configuration A runs sit closer to their mean
  11. Conclusion the mean of 12 minutes describes BETTER a typical execution in the first case, option c

The Proof, One Named Property Per Step

The reveal above computes the answer. This section establishes it, with every inference licensed by a property that holds independent of the problem. Method, direct proof. Compute both centers, establish they coincide, then compare the two dispersion measures.

Claim. Given set A equal to 9, 10, 11, 14, 16 and set B equal to 6, 8, 12, 15, 19, both sets share one mean, neither set is skewed, and the mean absolute deviation of A is strictly less than that of B.

  1. Definition of the arithmetic mean. The sum of the entries divided by their count. The mean of A equals the quantity 9 plus 10 plus 11 plus 14 plus 16, divided by 5.
  2. Associativity and commutativity of addition. Terms may be regrouped and reordered without changing a sum. That sum equals 60. Builds on line 1, since 9 plus 11 is 20, 10 plus 16 is 26, and 20 plus 26 plus 14 is 60.
  3. Cancellation law. For nonzero b, b times a over b equals a. The mean of A equals 12. Builds on lines 1 and 2, since 60 over 5 is 5 times 12 over 5.
  4. Definition of the arithmetic mean, as in line 1. The mean of B equals the quantity 6 plus 8 plus 12 plus 15 plus 19, divided by 5.
  5. Associativity and commutativity of addition, as in line 2. That sum equals 60. Builds on line 4, since 6 plus 19 is 25, 8 plus 12 is 20, and 25 plus 20 plus 15 is 60.
  6. Cancellation law, as in line 3. The mean of B equals 12. Builds on lines 4 and 5.
  7. Transitivity of equality. If a equals b and b equals c then a equals c. The two means are equal, each 12. Builds on lines 3 and 6.
  8. Definition of the median. For an ordered list of odd count 2k plus 1, the entry in position k plus 1. The median of A is 11 and the median of B is 12, since each list is already ordered with count 5.
  9. Trichotomy. Every real number is positive, negative, or zero. The mean minus the median is 1 for A and 0 for B. Builds on lines 3, 6, and 8. Neither set carries the one sided gap that marks skew, so the mean stands as the measure of center for both.
  10. Definition of the mean absolute deviation. The mean of the absolute differences between each entry and the mean, where the absolute value of x is x for nonnegative x and negative x otherwise. For A the differences are 3, 2, 1, 2, 4, summing to 12, so the mean absolute deviation is 2.4. Builds on line 3.
  11. Definition of the mean absolute deviation, as in line 10. For B the differences are 6, 4, 0, 3, 7, summing to 20, so the mean absolute deviation is 4. Builds on line 6.
  12. Order preserved under multiplication by a positive number. If a is less than b and c is positive then c times a is less than c times b. The mean absolute deviation of A is strictly less than that of B. Builds on lines 10 and 11, since 12 is less than 20 and multiplying each side by one fifth preserves the inequality.
  13. Modus ponens. If P implies Q and P holds then Q holds. The mean of A describes a typical value and the mean of B does not. Builds on lines 7, 9, and 12, under the criterion below.

Two flags. The threshold that separates a small gap from skew rests on inspection, not on a theorem, so line 9 records the two gaps exactly and the judgement that 1 out of 12 is small is a stated assumption. The criterion in line 13 is also an assumption. It reads as follows. When two sets share a mean and neither is skewed, the set with the smaller mean absolute deviation is the one whose mean describes a typical entry. No axiom licenses it.

Both sets have mean 12, neither is skewed, and the mean absolute deviation of A, at 2.4, is strictly less than that of B, at 4. The mean describes the typical entry in the first case only. QED.

A Conversation About the Answer

The same reasoning as a dialogue, since the wrong turns are as instructive as the proof. A student pushes on each claim and a science teacher answers.

Student. Set A, 9, 10, 11, 14, 16. Set B, 6, 8, 12, 15, 19. Same mean. Which one does 12 actually describe? I say the first. Are you sure about that choice?

Teacher. Sure. Both means are 12. Set A sits within 4 minutes of it, set B reaches 7 minutes away. The first case.

Student. So you are telling me set B is skewed.

Teacher. No. Set B is close to symmetric. Median 12, mean 12. The deviations offset, negative 6 against positive 7, negative 4 against positive 3. What separates the sets is width. Set A spans 7, set B spans 13.

Student. But you do think 12 describes typical behavior better in the first set than the second.

Teacher. Yes.

Student. Here is my read. Skew is what makes the median better than the mean, because extreme values drag the mean. Not the case here. Both means are 12, so the mean is fine and the question is just asking about the mean.

Teacher. Two ideas are mixed there. Skew decides mean against median, and you are right that there is no skew here. Spread decides whether the mean describes anything typical, and that is independent. Equal means do not make both means equally informative. The exercise gives identical means on purpose, so dispersion is the only remaining basis. Range 7 against range 13, average distance 2.4 against 4.

Student. Summarizing. Before, no skewed data, mean greater than median.

Teacher. Careful with the notation. With no skew the two are numerically equal, both 12. You mean prefer the mean over the median when the data is not skewed. Three lines. Skew picks the measure of center. Once the mean is the right measure, spread says how well it describes a typical case.

Student. Show me. And on the graph I could not tell which marker was moving, the mean or the median.

Teacher. Because nothing was pinned. Fix a line at the starting position and draw an arrow to each marker. The mean arrow is always the longer one.

Student. So once it is skewed, the median is the better description of what typically happens.

Teacher. Yes.

Student. And if one run comes back much longer, that skews the data and weakens the mean.

Teacher. Yes.

Student. Then average distance to the mean is one way to measure how skewed the data is.

Teacher. No.span That measures spread. It drops the sign, so it cannot say which side the tail is on. Your two sets are both symmetric and their average distances still differ, 2.4 against 4. For skew, compare the mean with the median. The gap gives you the size, the sign gives you the side.

Student. Can a graph be skewed toward both sides at once?

Teacher. No. Skew has one direction. Mass at both ends is not skew, the tails cancel and the mean lands near the median. That shape is heavy tailed, or bimodal if it has two peaks.

Student. Plain English. What is the average absolute deviation for?

Teacher. It answers one question. How far off is the average if you use it as your guess? For set A, 2.4 minutes. Guess 12 and you miss by about that much. For set B, 4. It rates the mean, it compares two sets that share a mean, and it keeps the unit of the data. Variance would hand you 8.8 minutes squared, which you cannot picture. Read it against the size of the mean, though. Missing by 2.4 on a mean of 12 is 20 percent.

Student. So each of the 5 numbers is the distance from each of the 6 runtimes to the mean, and their average is how far a typical runtime sits from it.

Teacher. The meaning is right. The count is not. Five runtimes, five distances.

Historical Background

Averaging repeated measurements became standard practice among eighteenth century astronomers, who took many sightings of the same star and needed one number to publish. The follow up question, how to score the leftover error, split the field. Laplace worked with the sum of absolute errors, while later practice moved to squared errors, and Ronald Fisher argued the case for the squared version in the early 1920s on grounds of sampling behavior.

Absolute deviation never left the working toolkit, because it keeps the unit of the data. An engineer reading 4 minutes understands it immediately, whereas 8.8 minutes squared describes nothing anyone can picture. Operations reporting, quality control, and forecast error tracking still lean on the absolute form for that reason.

Python Version

The script collects the runtimes for two configurations, reports the mean, the middle value, and the average distance from the mean for each, then names the configuration whose average can be trusted. 

# What this does: compares two sets of pipeline runtimes and says which average is trustworthy.
# Where it came from: a team ran the same pipeline on two cloud configurations and timed each run.
# The math idea: the average, the middle value, and how far the runs sit from the average.
# You type in: how many runs you timed, then each runtime for A and for B.
# It prints: the average and middle value for each set, and which average describes a typical run.

# This function adds up the runs and divides by how many there are.
def average(runs):
    return sum(runs) / len(runs)

# This function finds the middle value once the runs are in order.
def middle_value(runs):
    ordered = sorted(runs)
    count = len(ordered)
    # An odd count has one value in the middle.
    if count % 2 == 1:
        return ordered[count // 2]
    # An even count has two middle values, so average them.
    return (ordered[count // 2 - 1] + ordered[count // 2]) / 2

# This function measures how far the runs sit from the average, on average.
def average_distance(runs):
    center = average(runs)
    gaps = [abs(run - center) for run in runs]
    return sum(gaps) / len(gaps)

# This function collects the runtimes for one configuration.
def collect_runs(label, how_many):
    runs = []
    for number in range(1, how_many + 1):
        runs.append(float(input("Runtime of " + label + " run " + str(number) + " in minutes: ")))
    return runs

# Tidy up a number so whole values do not show a trailing zero.
def tidy(value):
    value = round(value, 2)
    return int(value) if float(value).is_integer() else value

# Tell the reader what we will figure out.
print("Let's see which configuration has a trustworthy average runtime.")

# Ask how many runs were timed on each configuration.
how_many = int(input("How many runs per configuration: "))

# Collect the runtimes for each configuration.
runs_a = collect_runs("configuration A", how_many)
runs_b = collect_runs("configuration B", how_many)

# Work out the three numbers for each set.
mean_a, mean_b = average(runs_a), average(runs_b)
middle_a, middle_b = middle_value(runs_a), middle_value(runs_b)
spread_a, spread_b = average_distance(runs_a), average_distance(runs_b)

# Show the numbers for each configuration.
print("Configuration A average", tidy(mean_a), "minutes, middle value", tidy(middle_a), "minutes, average distance", tidy(spread_a))
print("Configuration B average", tidy(mean_b), "minutes, middle value", tidy(middle_b), "minutes, average distance", tidy(spread_b))

# A big gap between the average and the middle value means one side has a long tail.
# When that happens the middle value is the better guide.
gap_a = abs(mean_a - middle_a)
gap_b = abs(mean_b - middle_b)
if gap_a > 0.1 * mean_a:
    print("Configuration A is lopsided, so use its middle value of", tidy(middle_a), "minutes instead.")
if gap_b > 0.1 * mean_b:
    print("Configuration B is lopsided, so use its middle value of", tidy(middle_b), "minutes instead.")

# When neither set is lopsided, the tighter set has the more useful average.
if spread_a < spread_b:
    print("Configuration A runs sit closer to its average, so that average describes a typical run.")
elif spread_b < spread_a:
    print("Configuration B runs sit closer to its average, so that average describes a typical run.")
else:
    print("Both sets are equally spread out, so neither average is a better guide.")

Open a terminal, start Python, paste the script, and type 5 for the count followed by the ten runtimes. It returns 12 minutes for both averages, 2.4 against 4 for the average distances, and it names Configuration A.
