THE PROJECT TEAM WENT ON LEAVE, THE CLOUD COMPUTE CREDIT MIX DID NOT

In this article ❧ Cloud spend arrives as one total, and finance has to charge it back by purchase type. The consumption mix, on demand against spot, tends to hold across months, because it follows how the workloads are built rather than how busy the month was. A team that knows the mix can charge back from the total alone. This problem shows how a mix stated in whole packs allocates a total measured in single credits, and how a few lines of Python make the allocation reusable for any total and any number of purchase types.

The Problem

A company draws down cloud compute credits on the last Friday of every month. Credits are sold in packs. This year, for every pack of on demand credits the company consumes, it consumes 2.5 packs of spot credits. One project consumed a total of only 2 and 1/10 credits, because most of the project team was on leave.

How many spot credits did this project consume?

  • a) one and a half
  • b) more than one
  • c) less than one
  • d) 1/2
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: a) one and a half ✅

Math Solution

A mix carries no unit, so a rate stated in packs holds for single credits without conversion. Writing the spot amount as 5 over 2 times the on demand amount and substituting into the total gives one equation in one unknown, which collects into 7 over 2 times the on demand amount equals 21 over 10, and then divides to 3 over 5 of an on demand credit and 3 over 2 spot credits.

Every credit the project draws carries 5 over 7 of itself into spot and 2 over 7 into on demand, so a total of 21 over 10 credits charges 3 over 2 to spot, whatever the project size.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Mix in fraction form: 2.5 equals 5 over 2, so spot credits s equals 5 over 2 times on demand credits d
  2. Total in fraction form: 2 and 1 over 10 equals 21 over 10, so d plus s equals 21 over 10
  3. Substitution: d plus 5 over 2 times d equals 21 over 10
  4. Multiplicative identity: 1 times d plus 5 over 2 times d equals 21 over 10
  5. Distributivity: the quantity 1 plus 5 over 2, times d, equals 21 over 10
  6. Common denominator rule: 2 over 2 plus 5 over 2 equals 7 over 2, so 7 over 2 times d equals 21 over 10
  7. Multiplication property of equality: both sides take the factor 2 over 7
  8. Multiplicative inverse and identity: the left side collapses to d
  9. Cancellation law: 2 times 21 over 7 times 10 equals 42 over 70 equals 3 over 5, so d equals 3 over 5
  10. Substitution into the mix: s equals 5 over 2 times 3 over 5
  11. Cancellation law: 15 over 10 equals 3 over 2, so s equals 3 over 2
  12. Verification: 3 over 5 plus 3 over 2 equals 6 over 10 plus 15 over 10 equals 21 over 10, and 3 over 2 divided by 3 over 5 equals 5 over 2
  13. Conclusion: the project consumed 3 over 2 spot credits, one and a half, and 3 over 5 of an on demand credit

Proof With Named Properties

The breakdown above states the moves. The units below state what licenses each move. Every step leads with a property, holds it in general form, then applies it to this problem's numbers.

Mode

Compute.

Setup

Let d be the on demand credits the project consumed and s the spot credits. The mix fixes spot against on demand at 5 over 2, so s equals 5 over 2 times d. The total fixes the credits consumed at 21 over 10, so d plus s equals 21 over 10.

Notation

  • d is the on demand credits consumed by this project, a positive rational, found in Step 10.
  • s is the spot credits consumed by this project, the unknown, a positive rational.
  • 5 over 2 is the mix of spot credits to on demand credits, the fraction form of 2.5. It carries no unit, so it holds for single credits as it holds for packs.
  • 21 over 10 is the total credits consumed, the fraction form of 2 and 1 over 10.

Step 1 — Substitution

Property. Substitution. Equals may replace equals.

Inference. d plus 5 over 2 times d equals 21 over 10. In the total equation, s is replaced by 5 over 2 times d.

Step 2 — Multiplicative identity

Property. Multiplicative identity. One times a equals a.

Inference. The left side equals 1 times d plus 5 over 2 times d. Builds on line 1. The bare d is written as 1 times d.

Step 3 — Distributivity of multiplication over addition

Property. Distributivity. a times c plus b times c equals the quantity a plus b, times c.

Inference. The left side equals the quantity 1 plus 5 over 2, times d. Builds on line 2. The shared factor d is collected.

Step 4 — Multiplicative identity, in fraction form

Property. Multiplicative identity, as in Step 2, with the cancellation law. For nonzero b, b over b equals 1.

Inference. 1 equals 2 over 2. Builds on line 3, rewriting the first addend over the denominator 2.

Step 5 — Addition of rational numbers with a common denominator

Property. Common denominator rule. a over c plus b over c equals the quantity a plus b, over c.

Inference. 2 over 2 plus 5 over 2 equals 7 over 2. Builds on line 4.

Step 6 — Substitution

Property. Substitution, as in Step 1.

Inference. The governing equation is 7 over 2 times d equals 21 over 10. Builds on lines 3 and 5.

Step 7 — Multiplication property of equality

Property. Multiplication property of equality. If a equals b then c times a equals c times b.

Inference. 2 over 7 times the quantity 7 over 2 times d equals 2 over 7 times 21 over 10. Builds on line 6. The same factor multiplies each side.

Step 8 — Multiplicative inverse and multiplicative identity

Property. Multiplicative inverse. A nonzero number times its inverse is 1. With multiplicative identity, 1 times a equals a.

Inference. The left side equals d. Builds on line 7. 2 over 7 times 7 over 2 is 1, and 1 times d is d.

Step 9 — Cancellation law

Property. Cancellation law. For nonzero b, b times a over b equals a.

Inference. The right side equals 3 over 5. Builds on line 7. 2 times 21 over 7 times 10 is 42 over 70, which is 14 times 3 over 14 times 5, and the 14 cancels.

Step 10 — Transitivity of equality

Property. Transitivity of equality. If a equals b and b equals c then a equals c.

Inference. Therefore d equals 3 over 5. Builds on lines 8 and 9.

Step 11 — Substitution

Property. Substitution, as in Step 1.

Inference. s equals 5 over 2 times 3 over 5. Builds on line 10, replacing d in the mix equation.

Step 12 — Cancellation law

Property. Cancellation law, as in Step 9.

Inference. s equals 3 over 2. Builds on line 11. 5 times 3 over 2 times 5 is 15 over 10, which is 5 times 3 over 5 times 2, and the 5 cancels.

Verification

Substitute into both constraints. The total, 3 over 5 plus 3 over 2, is 6 over 10 plus 15 over 10, which is 21 over 10. The mix, 3 over 2 divided by 3 over 5, is 3 over 2 times 5 over 3, which is 5 over 2. Both sides match.

Answer

The project consumed 3 over 2 spot credits, one and a half credits, and 3 over 5 of an on demand credit. Option a.

Properties in Action

Substitution and transitivity get no panel. They assemble and close the chain, they do not move the numbers.

Historical Background

Dividing a total into shares that follow a fixed pattern is one of the oldest recorded uses of arithmetic. Scribes in ancient Egypt worked out how to split a fixed quantity of bread and beer among workers of different rank, and the surviving Rhind Mathematical Papyrus carries problems of exactly this shape, a total handed out in unequal but stated proportions, with the answer written in unit fractions because the shares refuse to come out whole.

The commercial version arrived with medieval trade. Merchant manuals taught the rule of three and the rule of company, the second of which divided a venture's profit among partners in proportion to what each had put in, and Italian bookkeepers of the fourteenth century applied it to freight, insurance, and shared cargo. Cost accounting inherited the method as the allocation key, which charges a shared expense to departments in stated proportions, and cloud chargeback is the current form, splitting one metered total across purchase types, teams, and projects.

Python Version

The script splits any total across any number of named purchase types in stated proportions, using exact fractions so a fractional total like 21 over 10 never drifts into a rounding error.

from fractions import Fraction


def allocate_by_mix(total, mix):
    """Charge a metered total to purchase types that follow a fixed mix.

    total: the quantity to allocate, e.g. Fraction(21, 10) credits
    mix: a dict of name to weight, e.g. {"on_demand": 1, "spot": Fraction(5, 2)}

    The weights carry no unit, so a mix stated in packs applies to single credits.
    Returns a dict of name to exact share.
    """
    total = Fraction(total)
    weights = {name: Fraction(weight) for name, weight in mix.items()}
    denominator = sum(weights.values())
    if denominator == 0:
        raise ValueError("the mix weights must not sum to zero")
    return {name: total * weight / denominator for name, weight in weights.items()}


if __name__ == "__main__":
    shares = allocate_by_mix(
        Fraction(21, 10),
        {"on_demand": 1, "spot": Fraction(5, 2)},
    )
    for name, share in shares.items():
        print(f"{name}: {share} credits ({float(share)})")
    print("total:", sum(shares.values()))

Run it with python allocate_by_mix.py and it prints 3/5 of an on demand credit and 3/2 spot credits, with the shares summing back to 21/10. Change the total or add a third purchase type, e.g. reserved credits, and the same function returns the new chargeback.
