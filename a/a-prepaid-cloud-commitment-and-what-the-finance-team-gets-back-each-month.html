A PREPAID CLOUD COMMITMENT, AND WHAT THE FINANCE TEAM GETS BACK EACH MONTH

In this article ❧ Vendors quote incentives by the year and pay them by the billing cycle. That mismatch decides whether a prepaid commitment beats a pay as you go arrangement, and it has to be resolved before the contract is signed rather than after the first invoice arrives. This problem shows how a rate stated for one time unit is converted into the unit in which money actually moves, and why that conversion carries the whole decision.

The Problem

A finance manager moves 100,000 pesos into a prepaid cloud commitment account with a provider. The account pays a 3% annual credit rate, and the credit is posted to the account at the end of each month.

How much credit will the account have earned after 3 months?

  • a) 75
  • b) 750
  • c) 7.5
  • d) 7,500
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: b) 750 ✅

Math Solution

The annual rate and the posting frequency sit on different time bases, so the first move is to divide 3% by 12 to obtain the monthly rate of 0.25%. Then multiply the committed balance by that monthly rate to get one posting, and multiply again by 3 postings.

A 3% annual rate posted monthly is a 0.25% monthly rate, and the division into 12 has to happen before anything is multiplied.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Committed balance: 100,000 pesos, the amount the credit rate applies to
  2. Annual credit rate: 3%, or 0.03 as a decimal
  3. Monthly rate: 0.03 / 12 = 0.0025, or 0.25% per month
  4. Credit in one month: 100,000 × 0.0025 = 250 pesos per posting
  5. Credit over three postings: 250 × 3 = 750 pesos
  6. Conclusion: the account earns 750 pesos of cloud credit after 3 months, or 250 pesos per month

Historical Background

Turning a rate quoted for one span of time into the span in which payment lands is old commercial practice, and the 21st century rebuilt it from scratch. Between 2017 and 2023 regulators retired LIBOR, a benchmark published as a term figure for periods such as one month or three months, and the United States moved to SOFR, an overnight rate. Contracts written on a ready made term quote had to be rewritten, because borrowers and lenders now assemble the period figure themselves from daily observations, which is the same conversion a buyer performs when a vendor quotes a yearly incentive and pays it every month.
