30 ACCOUNTS IN 400 AND THE ONE SHARE THAT DRIVES CAPACITY PLANNING

In this article ❧ Survey work hands managers counts, and a count cannot travel. It cannot be compared with last quarter, applied to a larger population, or used as the baseline a program is meant to move. This problem shows how one division converts a raw count into a share of the sample, why that share is neither a ratio nor a rate, and how Python turns the operation into a reusable function.

The Problem

In a survey of 400 cloud accounts, 30 of them run 2 cost optimization reviews per week.

The relative frequency tells us that

  • a) 0.70% run 2 cost optimization reviews per week
  • b) 70% run fewer than 2 cost optimization reviews per week
  • c) 7.5% run more than 2 cost optimization reviews per week
  • d) 7.5% run 2 cost optimization reviews per week
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: d) 7.5% run 2 cost optimization reviews per week ✅

Math Solution

The category count sits in the numerator and the whole sample sits in the denominator, so the work is one division followed by one multiplication by 100. The three wrong options fail for two distinct reasons, one arithmetic and two about which category is being described.

Relative frequency cannot exceed 1 because the denominator contains the numerator, and that property is what makes the share comparable across surveys of different size.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Sample size: 400 surveyed cloud accounts, the denominator
  2. Category count: 30 accounts, the numerator
  3. Relative frequency: 30 ÷ 400 = 0.075
  4. Percentage form: 0.075 × 100 = 7.5%
  5. Option a check: 0.70% equals 0.007, about one tenth of the computed value, so it fails
  6. Option b check: 70% equals 0.70, and it describes accounts below 2 reviews, a category the survey never reported, so it fails
  7. Option c check: the number is right, the category is wrong, so it fails
  8. Option d check: 7.5% assigned to accounts running exactly 2 reviews per week matches the computation
  9. Conclusion: 7.5% of the surveyed accounts run 2 cost optimization reviews per week

Why This Math Matters

The survey gives a count, and a count alone cannot be compared across surveys of different size. Relative frequency converts that count into a share of the sample, which is the form a manager needs for capacity planning and targeting. A share of 7.5% can be applied to a population of 12,000 accounts to size the addressable group, compared against last quarter, or used as the baseline that a training program is meant to move. The count 30 does none of that work.

Relative Frequency, Ratio, and Rate

The three quantities all divide one number by another. What separates them is what sits in the denominator and whether the two quantities share units.

Relative frequency divides a part by the whole sample. The denominator contains the numerator, so the value cannot exceed 1, and the shares over all categories of one variable add to 1. A ratio divides one part by another part. The denominator excludes the numerator, so the value is unbounded. In this survey, 30 accounts against the other 370 gives 0.081, close to the relative frequency here but a different quantity that diverges as the share grows.

Rate divides quantities of different kinds. The 2 reviews per week inside the question is a rate, since reviews and weeks are different kinds of things, and neither number is a share of the other. Patrick Thompson notes that no conventional distinction between ratio and rate exists and that confusion about it is widespread, with one common convention treating a ratio as a comparison of quantities of like nature and a rate as a comparison of quantities of unlike nature.

Historical Background

The idea entered business practice before it entered mathematical theory. In 1662 John Graunt, a London haberdasher, bought the parish Bills of Mortality and worked out what proportion of all burials each cause of death accounted for. He noticed that some causes held a constant share of the whole year after year, and that observation of stability is the empirical claim on which the concept still rests. Karl Pearson later called him the first statistician.

Formalization took two more centuries. Robert Leslie Ellis and John Venn in nineteenth century Cambridge, then Richard von Mises in the 1920s, defined probability as the limit a relative frequency approaches over an indefinitely long sequence of observations. Von Mises added an axiom of convergence, that the proportion of outcomes stabilizes as trials accumulate, and an axiom of randomness. Kolmogorov later placed the whole subject on measure theoretic footing. The standing objection is practical, since nobody observes an infinite sequence.

So the 7.5% in this survey is a relative frequency, not a probability. It becomes an estimate of a probability only under an assumption about how the 400 accounts were drawn.

Python Version

The function returns the share in decimal and percentage form and rejects an empty sample, so it can be dropped into any survey pipeline where category counts arrive as integers.

def relative_frequency(count, total, digits=4):
    """Return the share of a sample that falls in one category.

    count -- observations in the category
    total -- observations in the whole sample
    """
    if total <= 0:
        raise ValueError("total must be greater than zero")
    if not 0 <= count <= total:
        raise ValueError("count must lie between zero and total")

    proportion = count / total
    return {
        "proportion": round(proportion, digits),
        "percentage": round(proportion * 100, digits - 2),
        "complement": round(1 - proportion, digits),
    }


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(relative_frequency(30, 400))
    # {'proportion': 0.075, 'percentage': 7.5, 'complement': 0.925}

Save it as relative_frequency.py and run python relative_frequency.py, which prints the proportion 0.075, the percentage 7.5, and the complement 0.925.
