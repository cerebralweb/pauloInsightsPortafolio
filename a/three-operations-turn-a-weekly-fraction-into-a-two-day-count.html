THREE OPERATIONS TURN A WEEKLY FRACTION INTO A TWO DAY COUNT

In this article ❧ Operations teams rarely finish a full workload in one burst. They commit to a share of it each week and spread that share evenly across the working days, which turns a fraction of a total into a steady daily rate. This problem shows how three plain operations move from a weekly fraction to a daily rate to the count for a chosen stretch of days, and how the same logic holds as code and as a written procedure.

The Problem

A technician services three quarters of the company laptops across five workdays. The company has forty laptops. How many will she service on Thursday and Friday?

  • a) 6
  • b) 2
  • c) 12
  • d) 16
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: c) 12 ✅

Math Solution

Take three quarters of forty to reach thirty, divide by five days to reach six per day, then multiply by two days to reach twelve.

The weekly fraction fixes the daily rate, and every later figure rides on that six per day.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Weekly amount: 30
  2. Daily rate: 6
  3. Two day count: 12

Proof

Each step states the finding and names the property that licenses it.

  1. Cancellation law. Three quarters of forty is thirty. Writing forty as four times ten, the four cancels against the denominator, leaving three times ten.
  2. Cancellation law. Thirty over five is six. Writing thirty as five times six, the five cancels, leaving six per day.
  3. Commutative law of multiplication. Six times two equals two times six. Order does not change the product.
  4. Associative law of multiplication. Six times two regroups as thirty times two over five, which is sixty over five, still twelve. Grouping does not change the product.

Reverse the chain to check. Twelve over two is six per day. Six times five is thirty across the week. Thirty over forty is three quarters. The fraction matches.

The Steps in Pictures

Each panel shows one equation with its property acting.

The planning question is simple. How much of the work lands on any given set of days. Answering it takes three moves. Take the fraction of the total, split that into a daily rate, then scale the rate to the days in view. A technician servicing three quarters of forty laptops across five workdays handles thirty in the week, six each day, and twelve across Thursday and Friday.

The order matters. The fraction of the total comes first, because the daily rate has no meaning until the weekly amount is fixed. Only then does the split into days make sense, and only then can the rate be scaled to the days you care about.

# What this does: works out how many laptops get serviced over a chosen number of days.
# Where it came from: a technician services part of the laptops each week, spread evenly across the workdays.
# The math idea: take a fraction of a total, split it evenly per day, then multiply by the days you care about.
# You type in: the total laptops, the fraction top and bottom, the workdays per week, and the days you want.
# It prints: how many laptops get serviced across those days.

# This function does the math.
def laptops_serviced(total, frac_top, frac_bottom, workdays, days_wanted):
    # Take the fraction of the total. So 3 over 4 of 40.
    weekly = total * frac_top / frac_bottom
    # Split the weekly amount evenly across the workdays.
    per_day = weekly / workdays
    # Scale the daily amount to the days you asked about.
    return per_day * days_wanted

# Tell the reader what we will figure out.
print("Let's find how many laptops get serviced over a set number of days.")

# Ask for the total laptops.
total = float(input("Total laptops: "))

# Ask for the top of the fraction.
frac_top = float(input("Fraction top (e.g. 3): "))

# Ask for the bottom of the fraction.
frac_bottom = float(input("Fraction bottom (e.g. 4): "))

# Ask for the workdays per week.
workdays = float(input("Workdays per week: "))

# Ask for the days you want counted.
days_wanted = float(input("Days in question: "))

# Do the math.
result = laptops_serviced(total, frac_top, frac_bottom, workdays, days_wanted)

# Show a whole number when it lands on one.
result = int(result) if result.is_integer() else result

# Print the answer with words that fit the scenario.
print("Laptops serviced across those days:", result)

# Fraction Daily Rate Scaler

This skill answers a common word problem shape. Someone handles a fraction of a total each week, spread evenly across the workdays, and you want the count for a smaller set of days. It takes the total, the fraction top and bottom, the workdays per week, and the days in question, then returns how many items fall in those days. It works for any numbers, not just one problem.

---

## Dependencies

None required for the plain path. Basic arithmetic covers it.

Optional, for exact results when numbers do not divide evenly: an exact fraction tool from the standard library. Use it only when a clean decimal is not guaranteed.

---

## Inputs

- **Total items**: the whole count, e.g. 40 laptops.
- **Fraction top**: the numerator of the weekly share, e.g. 3.
- **Fraction bottom**: the denominator of the weekly share, e.g. 4.
- **Workdays per week**: how many days the weekly share is spread across, e.g. 5.
- **Days in question**: how many of those days you want counted, e.g. 2.

---

## Steps

### Step 1 — Take the fraction of the total

Multiply the total by the fraction top, then divide by the fraction bottom. This gives the weekly amount handled. With 40 and three quarters, the weekly amount is 30.

### Step 2 — Find the daily rate

Divide the weekly amount by the workdays per week. This gives the amount handled per day. With 30 across 5 days, the daily rate is 6.

### Step 3 — Scale to the days in question

Multiply the daily rate by the days in question. This gives the answer. With 6 per day across 2 days, the result is 12.

### Step 4 — Present the result

Show the count with plain text tied to the scenario, not a bare number. If the result lands on a whole number, display it as a whole number. If it does not divide evenly and precision matters, keep it as an exact fraction rather than a rounded decimal.
