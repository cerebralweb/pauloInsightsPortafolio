WHAT A CLOUD TEAM READS OFF TWO PRINTS OF THE SAME FIBER ROUTE

In this article ❧ Infrastructure teams plan physical work from documents that never carry the physical unit they need. A route drawing states a scale factor, and the factor counts units of ground behind units of paper, so cost, splice count, and latency all wait on a conversion before anyone can order a build. This problem shows how a drawing of a drawing hides a second ratio, and how a short Python function turns that reasoning into a tool the team can reuse on any pair of prints.

The Problem

Marisol and Diego are planning a fiber link between two availability zones in the same cloud region. Each printed the region route map at a different size. The same fiber segment appears as a line 26 cm long on Marisol's print and 4 cm long on Diego's print. The scale of Diego's print relative to the actual route is 1 to 563,270.

Approximately how many meters of actual route does each centimeter on Marisol's print represent?

  • a) 866
  • b) 896
  • c) 906
  • d) 836
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: a) 866 ✅

Math Solution

Diego's print fixes the real length of the segment, so multiply his 4 cm by the scale factor 563,270 to get 2,253,080 real centimeters. Then divide that length by Marisol's 26 cm and convert the result to meters.

A scale factor counts units, never centimeters, so a print that shows the segment 6.5 times longer carries a factor 6.5 times smaller.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Real route length: (4)(563,270) = 2,253,080 real cm
  2. Real centimeters per centimeter of Marisol's print: 2,253,080 / 26 = 86,656.92 cm
  3. Conversion to meters: 86,656.92 / 100 = 866.57 m
  4. Reported value: 866 m per centimeter, option a
  5. Cross check without the route length: 26 / 4 = 6.5, then 563,270 / 6.5 = 86,656.92 cm, the same 866.57 m

Property by Property Solution

The breakdown above gives the arithmetic. This version names the property that licenses every move, so each line rests on a result that holds independent of this route.

Setup

Diego's print carries a stated scale factor, so it fixes the real length of the segment. Marisol's print shows that same real length across a different paper length. The scale factor relation gives d = kDLD and d = kMLM. The unit relation gives u = kM/100.

Notation

  • d is the real length of the fiber segment in centimeters, fixed in Step 1.
  • kD is the scale factor of Diego's print, 563,270, a count of real centimeters per centimeter of paper, not a length.
  • LD is the segment length on Diego's print, 4 centimeters.
  • kM is the scale factor of Marisol's print, a count of real centimeters per centimeter of paper, fixed in Step 6.
  • LM is the segment length on Marisol's print, 26 centimeters.
  • u is the meters of route per centimeter of Marisol's print, the unknown, a positive rational.
  • 100 is the centimeters in one meter.
  • 1/26 is the multiplicative inverse of 26, and 1/1300 the multiplicative inverse of 1300.
  • x, y, c are any numbers, used to state a property in general form.
  • ⇒ reads implies, and ≈ reads is approximately equal to.
  • A fraction bar and the sign / both read divided by, and adjacent parentheses read multiplied by, so (2)(5) is 10.

Solution

  1. Substitution. Property. x = y ⇒ x may replace y. Inference. d = kDLD with kD = 563,270 and LD = 4, so d = (563,270)(4) = 2,253,080.
  2. Transitivity of equality. Property. x = y and y = z ⇒ x = z. Inference. kMLM = d and d = 2,253,080, so 26kM = 2,253,080. From line 1.
  3. Multiplication property of equality. Property. x = y ⇒ cx = cy. Inference. (1/26)(26kM) = (1/26)(2,253,080). From line 2.
  4. Multiplicative inverse and multiplicative identity. Property. For x ≠ 0, (1/x)x = 1 and 1x = x. Inference. (1/26)(26kM) = (1)kM = kM. From line 3.
  5. Cancellation law. Property. For y ≠ 0, (yx)/y = x. Inference. (1/26)(2,253,080) = 2,253,080/26 = (2)(1,126,540)/(2)(13) = 1,126,540/13.
  6. Transitivity of equality, as in Step 2. Inference. kM = 1,126,540/13. From lines 4 and 5.
  7. Substitution, as in Step 1. Inference. u = kM/100 with kM = 1,126,540/13, so u = 1,126,540/1300. From line 6.
  8. Cancellation law, as in Step 5. Inference. u = 1,126,540/1300 = (20)(56,327)/(20)(65) = 56,327/65 = 866 + 37/65. From line 7.
  9. FLAG, rounding to the nearest integer. No standard named property licenses this move. It rests on the stated assumption that the answer is reported as a whole number of meters. Inference. 37/65 ≈ 0.57, so u ≈ 866.57 and the nearest whole meter below is 866. From line 8.

Verification

kM = 100u = (100)(56,327/65) = 5,632,700/65.
kMLM = (5,632,700/65)(26) = (5,632,700)(2)/5 = 2,253,080.
kDLD = (563,270)(4) = 2,253,080.
Both sides match at 2,253,080.

The Same Ground, Spread Thinner

Forget the scale factor for a moment. Forget the units. There is one thing here, a stretch of ground with fiber in it, and two pictures of that thing. Nobody cares about the pictures. The pictures are shadows.

Here is the whole idea. Both sheets show the same segment, so whatever length each sheet gives it, both lengths stand for one and the same stretch of earth. That is the only fact in the problem. The rest is bookkeeping.

Now put the two sheets side by side. The segment runs 4 across one and 26 across the other. Marisol spent 6.5 times as much paper on the same ground. She did not find more ground. She spread it thinner.

And that is all a scale tells you, how much ground gets packed into one step of your finger. Pack the same ground into 6.5 times as many steps and each step must carry 6.5 times less. It has to. If a step on Marisol's sheet carried as much as a step on Diego's, her segment would run 6.5 times farther than reality, and it does not, because it is the same segment.

So her scale is Diego's scale shrunk by 6.5. Share 563,270 among 6.5 and you get about 86,657. Those are centimeters, because Diego's number counted centimeters of ground for a centimeter of paper, and shrinking a count of centimeters leaves centimeters. A hundred of them make a meter. A bit under 867 meters.

Notice what never happened. The length of the fiber route was never computed. Why would it be? The question asks what one step of Marisol's finger is worth, and for that you only need to know how her steps compare with Diego's. The route length is a number the problem dangles in front of you and does not need.

Drag the slider below. The bar grows, the ground behind it does not, and each step of the finger pays for it.

Slide it to 4 and the two bars agree, because the same ground on the same amount of paper leaves the scale alone. Slide it to 40 and each centimeter drops to about 563 meters. The ground never moved.

A Conversation About Dividing by a Half

What follows is an imagined exchange, in the spirit of the author of A Mathematician's Lament, about a doubt that surfaced while writing the proof above.

Paulo. Something bothers me. Divide a number by a rational under 1 and the quotient comes out much larger than the dividend. The multiplicative inverse makes the arithmetic quick, fine. But what is the rationale.

Lockhart. Before rationale, a question. What are you asking for when you divide 10 by a half.

Paulo. The result of an operation.

Lockhart. That tells me nothing. You are asking how many halves fit in 10. Halves are small. Of course a lot of them fit. There is your large number, and no rule was needed to produce it. Drag the thing below and count.

1/2

Paulo. Accepted. But why must the inverse of a small number be big. That still feels like a coincidence I am asked to memorize.

Lockhart. Nothing to memorize. Draw a rectangle with area exactly 1. Make it narrow and watch what the height is obliged to do. It has no choice.

x = 2.0

Paulo. So the inverse is a way to erase an awkward number and put an easy one in its place, and because the replacement is the opposite, the relationship flips, division becomes multiplication.

Lockhart. Half right, and the wrong half is the interesting one. Erasing is a fine word when there is an unknown hiding under the number. You had 26k = 2,253,080 and you wanted k alone. Multiply by 1/26 and the left side reads 1k. Nothing is gone yet, mind you. A product sits there.

Paulo. Then the identity property drops the 1.

Lockhart. That is the exit door. Without it the line stops at 1k forever. But your second claim, that division becomes multiplication, gets the story backwards. It never became anything. Division by x is multiplication by 1/x. That is what the word means. What flips is not the operation, only the size of the factor.

Paulo. Why do you keep saying coefficient. My example, 2M divided by a half, has no variable in it.

Lockhart. Because I was still standing inside your proof, where 26 sat on top of an unknown. Your example is different and you are right to separate them. 2M divided by a half is finished arithmetic. There is nobody to isolate, so nothing is erased. Ask instead how many halves fit in 2M, and the answer is 4M, which the first picture would have told you.

Paulo. Then in 10 divided by a half, am I multiplying the half by 2. Or the whole expression by 2, which would give 20 anyway.

Lockhart. Neither, and notice how the right answer arrived through a wrong route. Both of those moves change the value. What happens is a rename. 10 divided by a half and 10 times 2 are two names for one number. If you insist on a derivation, call the answer q. A quotient times its divisor gives the dividend, so q times a half is 10. Multiply both sides by 2 and the left becomes q times 1, then q. The right becomes 20. There you did multiply both sides, but that lives inside a proof about q. The original expression was left untouched.

Paulo. So the inverse manufactures a 1, and the identity takes it away.

Lockhart. Two properties, one after the other, and neither is a trick. The first says every nonzero number has a partner that pairs with it to give 1. The second says 1 is the number that leaves everything alone. Put them in sequence and an unknown walks out by itself.

Historical Background

Copying a drawing at a chosen ratio became a trade skill once Christoph Scheiner described the pantograph in 1603. Its jointed arms traced an original and produced a larger or smaller version, which meant a copy of a copy compounded two ratios through the linkage rather than through arithmetic on paper. Draftsmen, engravers, and mint workers relied on it for two centuries.

The reverse operation carried commercial weight. Nineteenth century survey offices reduced field plans to publication scale before printing, and railway promoters, canal companies, and telegraph builders read distances and costs straight off those reduced sheets. A mistaken scale factor priced a route wrongly, which is the same exposure a network team carries when it reads a fiber run off a print.

Python Version

The script takes any reference print with a known scale factor and returns the meters of ground behind one centimeter of a second print of the same object, so it works for any pair of drawings, not just this route.

def meters_per_cm(reference_length_cm, reference_scale, target_length_cm):
    """Meters of real distance per centimeter on a second print.
 
    reference_length_cm : the feature length on the print whose scale is known
    reference_scale     : real units per print unit on that print, e.g. 563270
    target_length_cm    : the same feature length on the print being read
    """
    real_cm = reference_length_cm * reference_scale
    real_cm_per_target_cm = real_cm / target_length_cm
    return real_cm_per_target_cm / 100
 
 
if __name__ == "__main__":
    value = meters_per_cm(4, 563270, 26)
    print(round(value, 2))       # 866.57
    print(round(value))          # 866

Run it with python scale.py and it prints 866.57, the meters of fiber route standing behind each centimeter of Marisol's print, plus the rounded value 866 used as the answer.
