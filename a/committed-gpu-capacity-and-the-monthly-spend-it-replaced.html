COMMITTED GPU CAPACITY AND THE MONTHLY SPEND IT REPLACED

In this article ❧ Cloud contracts and operating budgets speak in different periods. A vendor quotes a year, a finance team reviews a month, and a saving stated per month sits between the two. This problem shows how one annual figure and one stated saving recover the spend a team left behind, and how naming the property behind each step keeps the chain auditable.

The Problem

Sandra runs the MLOps team at a fintech firm. The team retrains its fraud detection models on a fixed weekly schedule, so monthly GPU spend stays steady. She moved the workload from on demand GPU instances to a one year committed capacity plan, which saves 3,500 dollars per month. The committed plan is billed once a year at 294,000 dollars.

How much was the team paying per month on on demand instances?

  • a) 21,000
  • b) 27,000
  • c) 24,500
  • d) 28,000
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: d) 28,000 ✅

Math Solution

The annual bill of 294,000 dollars covers 12 equal periods, so divide it by 12 to get the committed monthly cost of 24,500 dollars. The switch lowered the monthly figure by 3,500 dollars, so add that saving back to reach the previous spend of 28,000 dollars.

The saving is stated per month while the contract is priced per year, so the periods must agree before the two figures can meet.

Step by Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

Mode. Compute.

Setup. Let A be the annual billing of the committed GPU plan, c the monthly cost of that plan, s the monthly saving from the switch, and p the monthly on demand spend before the switch. The year holds 12 equal monthly periods, so 12c = A, with A fixed at 294,000. The saving fixes p − c = s, with s fixed at 3,500. Solve for p.

Notation.

  • A is the annual billing of the committed plan, 294,000 dollars.
  • c is the monthly cost of the committed plan, 24,500 dollars once Step 5 fixes it.
  • s is the monthly saving from the switch, 3,500 dollars.
  • p is the monthly on demand spend before the switch, the unknown, in dollars.
  • 12 is the count of monthly billing periods in one year.
  • 1/12 is the multiplicative inverse of 12.
  • ⇒ reads implies.
  1. Substitution. Property. x = y ⇒ x may replace y. Inference. 12c = A and A = 294,000, so 12c = 294,000.
  2. Multiplication property of equality. Property. x = y ⇒ kx = ky. Inference. (1/12)(12c) = (1/12)(294,000). From line 1.
  3. Multiplicative inverse and identity. Property. For x ≠ 0, (1/x)x = 1 and 1x = x. Inference. (1/12)(12c) = (1)c = c. From line 2.
  4. Cancellation law. Property. For y ≠ 0, (yx)/y = x. Inference. (1/12)(294,000) = 294,000/12 = (12)(24,500)/12 = 24,500.
  5. Transitivity of equality. Property. x = y and y = z ⇒ x = z. Inference. c = 24,500. From lines 3 and 4.
  6. Substitution, again. Property. As in Step 1. Inference. p − c = s, c = 24,500, s = 3,500, so p − 24,500 = 3,500. From line 5.
  7. Addition property of equality. Property. x = y ⇒ x + k = y + k. Inference. (p − 24,500) + 24,500 = 3,500 + 24,500. From line 6.
  8. Additive inverse and identity. Property. x + (−x) = 0 and x + 0 = x. Inference. p = 28,000. From line 7.

Verification. 12(24,500) = 294,000. 28,000 − 24,500 = 3,500. Both constraints match.

Answer. p = 28,000, option d. The team was paying 28,000 dollars per month on on demand GPU instances.

Historical Background

Splitting a yearly sum into equal periods is one of the oldest accounting habits on record. Medieval estate stewards and later the double entry bookkeepers of Renaissance Italy divided annual rents, tithes, and wages into monthly and quarterly charges so that a ledger could be read against a season rather than a year.

The same move sits under modern subscription finance. Insurers priced annual premiums as monthly installments, telephone carriers billed yearly service in monthly cycles, and cloud vendors now quote committed capacity for a year while their customers report a month at a time. The arithmetic did not change. Only the asset did, from farmland to GPU hours.
