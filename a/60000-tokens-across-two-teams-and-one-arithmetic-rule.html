60,000 TOKENS ACROSS TWO TEAMS AND ONE ARITHMETIC RULE

In this article ❧ AI token budgets cap how much each team can run against a model, so a finance owner rolling up monthly usage has to combine allocations that differ in size. When one team holds several times the budget of another, the smaller share can still swing the combined total, which makes the order of each operation matter. This problem shows how one pass of multiplication, a fraction, and addition settles a shared usage figure, and how each step rests on a named arithmetic property.

The Problem

A product team is allocated 33,000 in AI tokens each month. This month it used 16,000 tokens on a customer chatbot. A second team was allocated four times as many tokens, and it used one-third of its allocation on document summarization.

How many tokens did the two teams use together this month?

  • a) 45,200
  • b) 43,642
  • c) 60,000
  • d) 44,000
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: c) 60,000 ✅

Math Solution

The second team allocation is four times 33,000, which is 132,000. Take one-third of that for its usage, 44,000, then add the first team usage of 16,000.

The second team spends only a third of its budget, yet that third alone is larger than the first team's entire outlay.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. First team usage: 16,000 tokens
  2. Second team allocation: 4 × 33,000 = 132,000 tokens
  3. Second team usage: 132,000 ÷ 3 = 44,000 tokens
  4. Total usage: 16,000 + 44,000 = 60,000 tokens

Proof with Named Properties

Each step below states the property that licenses it, then applies that property to the numbers of this problem. Let T be the total tokens the two teams used together. T equals 16,000 plus one-third of four times 33,000.

  1. Associative law of multiplication. For any numbers, a times b times c equals a times the quantity b times c. So one-third of four times 33,000 equals four times one-third of 33,000, and the second team usage is 4 times 33,000 over 3.
  2. Cancellation law. For nonzero b, b times a over b equals a. So 33,000 over 3 equals 11,000, since 33,000 is 3 times 11,000 and the 3 cancels. Builds on step 1.
  3. Substitution. Equals may replace equals. So the second team usage is 4 times 11,000. Builds on step 2.
  4. Closure under multiplication. The product of two integers is an integer. So the second team usage is 44,000, since 4 times 11,000 is 44,000. Builds on step 3.
  5. Closure under addition. The sum of two integers is an integer. So T equals 16,000 plus 44,000, which is 60,000. Builds on step 4.

Verification. The second team allocation is 4 times 33,000, which is 132,000. One-third of 132,000 is 44,000. Add the first team usage of 16,000. The sum is 60,000. The answer is c). QED.

Mathematical Background

The problem chains three basic operations on whole numbers and a fraction. Multiplication scales a quantity up by a whole factor. Taking one-third is division into three equal parts. Addition combines the results. The order matters because each step feeds the next. A useful conceptual point is that these operations sit inside a fixed structure where multiplication distributes over addition, so partial spends can be summed in any grouping and still reach the same total. In the 20th century this kind of ordered arithmetic became the base layer of digital computing. The 1945 report on the EDVAC by John von Neumann described a machine that stored numbers and instructions together and executed arithmetic step by step, the pattern that still underlies the token accounting in the problem.
