WHEN A DISCOUNT AND A TAX LAND ON THE SAME INVOICE LINE

In this article ❧ Prices rarely arrive clean. A published amount picks up a rebate, a levy, a surcharge, and the party issuing the document decides which adjustment lands where. Disputes over that sequence fill contracts, invoices, and audit notes, because two people can agree on every percentage and still argue about the base each one applies to. This problem shows how the wording of a pricing clause, not the arithmetic, settles the question, and how a short function turns the rule into something reusable.

The Problem

María and Mariana traveled to Miami and bought a pair of boots at a 25% discount. A 6% tax was added to the final price. Mariana says the discount is calculated first and the tax second. María says the tax is calculated first and the discount second. The rule in force is that the tax must be calculated on the original price, which was 200, and the discount on the amount that results.

Which one is correct, and what is the final price?

  • a) Mariana, 159
  • b) Both are correct, 159
  • c) María, 159
  • d) Mariana, 162
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: c) 159 ✅

Math Solution

The rule fixes the tax base at the original price, so the sequence runs 200 × 1.06 = 212 and then 212 × 0.75 = 159, which is María's order. The reverse sequence returns the same amount, 200 × 0.75 × 1.06 = 159, because both adjustments enter as factors and factors may be reordered freely.

Three of the four options carry the same amount, so the item turns on the pricing clause rather than on the arithmetic.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Original price, 200
  2. Tax on the original price, 200 × 0.06 = 12, so the taxed amount is 212
  3. Discount on the taxed amount, 212 × 0.25 = 53
  4. Final price, 212 − 53 = 159
  5. Reverse order check, 200 × 0.75 = 150, then 150 × 1.06 = 159
  6. Why option d fails, 200 + 12 − 50 = 162 takes both adjustments on the original price and nets them, which is a different rule
  7. Who is correct, María, since the clause taxes the original price and discounts the taxed amount

Proof

Mode. Compute.

Setup. Let P be the original price, d the discount rate, and t the tax rate. The purchase fixes P at 200, d at 0.25, and t at 0.06. Let F be the final price. Mariana applies the discount first and the tax second. María applies the tax first and the discount second. The clause fixes the tax base at the original price and the discount base at the amount that results.

Notation.

  • P, the original price, 200
  • d, the discount rate, 0.25
  • t, the tax rate, 0.06
  • F, the final price, the unknown, a positive rational
  • F₁, the final price under Mariana's order, produced in Step 3
  • F₂, the final price under María's order, produced in Step 5
  • ⇒ reads implies
  • ≠ reads is not equal to

Step 1. Distributivity of multiplication over addition
Property. Distributivity. xy − xz = x(y − z).
Inference. P − dP = P(1 − d) = 200(0.75) = 150.

Step 2. Distributivity of multiplication over addition, as in Step 1
Property. Distributivity. xy + xz = x(y + z).
Inference. P + tP = P(1 + t) = 200(1.06) = 212.

Step 3. Substitution
Property. Substitution. x = y ⇒ x may replace y.
Inference. F₁ = (P(1 − d))(1 + t) = 150(1.06) = 159. From line 1.

Step 4. Commutativity and associativity of multiplication
Property. Commutativity and associativity. xy = yx and (xy)z = x(yz).
Inference. (P(1 − d))(1 + t) = P(1 − d)(1 + t) = P(1 + t)(1 − d) = (P(1 + t))(1 − d).

Step 5. Substitution, as in Step 3
Property. Substitution. x = y ⇒ x may replace y.
Inference. F₂ = (P(1 + t))(1 − d) = 212(0.75) = 159. From line 2.

Step 6. Transitivity of equality
Property. Transitivity of equality. x = y and y = z ⇒ x = z.
Inference. F₁ = 159 and F₂ = 159 ⇒ F₁ = F₂. From lines 3 and 5.

Step 7. FLAG, order fixed by a stated assumption
Property. None. This move rests on the clause stated in Setup, not on a property of arithmetic.
Inference. Tax base P and discount base P(1 + t) ⇒ the required order is tax then discount, which is María's, so F = F₂ = 159. From line 5.

Verification. Mariana's order, 200(0.75) = 150 and 150(1.06) = 159. María's order, 200(1.06) = 212 and 212(0.75) = 159. Both sides match, so F = 159.

Answer. F = 159, option c. María states the order the clause requires, although the two orders return the same amount by Step 4. Option d, 162, comes from P + tP − dP = 200 + 12 − 50, which takes both adjustments on P rather than composing them, and P(1 + t)(1 − d) ≠ P + tP − dP.

Historical Background

Charges stated in hundredths reach back to imperial Rome, where the treasury collected the centesima rerum venalium, a levy of one part in a hundred on goods sold at auction. Roman clerks kept such charges in hundredths because the base was easy to restate and easy to audit.

The habit spread through Mediterranean trade. Italian merchants of the fifteenth century wrote per cento into their ledgers as a standing shorthand for freight, brokerage, and duty, and the sequence in which those charges hit a consignment became a matter for the contract rather than the clerk. The dispute in this problem is the same dispute, carried into a shop in Miami.

Python Version

The script states the pricing rule once as a product of factors, so any pair of discount and tax rates can be priced without rewriting the logic.

def final_price(price, discount_rate, tax_rate):
    """Return the price after a discount and a tax, in either order."""
    return price * (1 - discount_rate) * (1 + tax_rate)


def netted_price(price, discount_rate, tax_rate):
    """Return the price when both adjustments are taken on the original base."""
    return price + price * tax_rate - price * discount_rate


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(final_price(200, 0.25, 0.06))   # 159.0
    print(netted_price(200, 0.25, 0.06))  # 162.0

Run it with python pricing.py and it returns 159.0 for the sequential rule and 162.0 for the netted rule, which is the gap the answer options are built around.
