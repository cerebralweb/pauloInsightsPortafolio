THE CREDIT BALANCE IS FIXED, THE INVOICE QUEUE IS NOT

In this article ❧ Cloud platforms bill compute in fractions of a unit, while the work a team cares about is counted in whole items. That mismatch is where capacity planning lives, because a balance figure says nothing about throughput until it is converted into the number of runs it funds. This problem shows how a division and a unit rate turn a spending balance into a firm output commitment, and how every step rests on a property that holds independent of the scenario.

The Problem

Luisa runs an invoice processing pipeline on serverless functions. Every 1/3 of a compute credit processes 5 invoices.

How many invoices can she process with 5 and 2/3 compute credits?

  • a) 85
  • b) 84
  • c) 83
  • d) 83
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: a) 85 ✅

Math Solution

Rewrite the balance as a single fraction, then divide it by the credits one block consumes to count the blocks the balance funds. Multiply that count by the invoices one block clears.

The balance buys 17 blocks because 1/3 fits three times in every whole credit, and each block is worth 5 invoices.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Whole part as a fraction: 5 equals 5/1, and 5/1 equals 15/3
  2. Balance as one fraction: 15/3 plus 2/3 equals 17/3 credits
  3. Blocks funded: 17/3 divided by 1/3 equals 17/3 times 3, which is 17 blocks
  4. Invoices per block: 5 invoices
  5. Total throughput: 17 times 5 equals 85 invoices
  6. Check: 17 blocks consume 17/3 credits, the full balance

Why This Math Matters for the Business Problem

The pipeline bills compute in fractions of a credit, but the output is counted in whole invoices. Division by a fraction converts a credit balance into the number of processing blocks the balance funds. Multiplying by the block yield converts blocks into throughput.

Without that conversion, Luisa can see her balance and her unit rate but cannot state capacity. With it, she can size a batch before running it, decide whether the current balance clears the queue, and price processing per invoice for a client.

The Math Structure

  • Topic: division by a fraction, then scaling by a unit rate
  • Compute consumed per processing block: 1/3 of a credit
  • Invoices produced per block: 5
  • Total credits available: 5 and 2/3
  • Unknown: total invoices processed
Where the scenario came from

The arithmetic is lifted from a classroom problem. Luisa uses 1/3 of a cup of flour to make 5 cinnamon rolls, and the question asks how many rolls she can make with 5 and 2/3 cups. Cups become credits and rolls become invoices, with the numbers untouched.

Candidate business settings were ranked before one was chosen.

  1. Serverless functions: compute is billed in small fractional credits, and each fraction maps to a fixed output volume
  2. Batch inference: a fixed slice of GPU time processes a fixed number of records
  3. Container orchestration: pods take fractional CPU allocations, though output counts feel less natural
  4. Document extraction: a token budget per document works, but the fraction reads as a limit rather than a rate
  5. Cloud storage: fractional terabytes hold countable objects, yet nothing is produced
  6. Cloud cost management: credits per job fits the arithmetic but the scenario stays vague

Serverless functions ranked first, so the scenario was built there.

The Proof, Step by Step

Mode

Compute.

Setup

Let b be the number of processing blocks the credit balance funds, and n the invoices processed. The balance is 5 and 2/3 credits. Each block consumes 1/3 of a credit, so the credits spent on b blocks are 1/3 times b. Spending the full balance gives 1/3 times b equals 5 and 2/3. Each block clears 5 invoices, so n equals 5 times b.

Notation

  • c is the credit balance available, 5 and 2/3, rewritten as 17/3 in Step 3
  • b is the count of processing blocks the balance funds, fixed at 17 in Step 7
  • n is the invoices processed, the unknown, a positive integer
  • 1/3 is the credits one block consumes
  • 5 is the invoices one block clears
  • k is the common factor applied to a numerator and a denominator in Step 2, here 3

Step 1 — Embedding of the integers in the rationals

Property. Every integer m equals m over 1.

Inference. 5 equals 5/1.

Step 2 — Fundamental property of fractions

Property. For nonzero k, a over b equals ka over kb.

Inference. 5 equals 15/3. Builds on line 1, with k equal to 3.

Step 3 — Addition of fractions with a common denominator

Property. For a common denominator d, a over d plus e over d equals the quantity a plus e, over d.

Inference. c equals 17/3. Builds on line 2. Fifteen thirds plus two thirds is seventeen thirds.

Step 4 — Multiplication property of equality

Property. If a equals b then c times a equals c times b.

Inference. 3 times the quantity 1/3 times b equals 3 times 17/3. Builds on line 3. The same factor multiplies each side of 1/3 times b equals 17/3.

Step 5 — Multiplicative inverse and identity

Property. A nonzero number times its inverse is one, and one times a equals a.

Inference. The left side equals b. Builds on line 4. Three times one third is one, and one times b is b.

Step 6 — Cancellation law

Property. For nonzero d, d times a over d equals a.

Inference. The right side equals 17. Builds on line 4. Three times seventeen thirds is 3 times 17 over 3, and the 3 cancels, leaving 17.

Step 7 — Transitivity of equality

Property. If a equals b and b equals c then a equals c.

Inference. b equals 17. Builds on lines 5 and 6.

Step 8 — Substitution

Property. Equals may replace equals.

Inference. n equals 5 times 17. Builds on line 7, replacing b in n equals 5 times b.

Step 9 — Distributive law

Property. a times the quantity x plus y equals a times x plus a times y.

Inference. n equals 85. Builds on line 8. Five times seventeen is 5 times 10 plus 5 times 7, which is 50 plus 35.

Verification

Seventeen blocks consume 17 times 1/3, which is 17/3, or 5 and 2/3 credits. That is the full balance. Those blocks clear 17 times 5, which is 85.

Answer

The balance of 5 and 2/3 credits funds 17 processing blocks and clears 85 invoices, option a.

Mathematical Background

Dividing by a fraction asks how many copies of that fraction fit inside a quantity. Since 1/3 fits three times in every whole unit, 17/3 credits contain 17 thirds. The standard method inverts the divisor and multiplies, which is why 17/3 divided by 1/3 becomes 17/3 times 3.

The second operation is a unit rate applied to a count, so the two steps stay separate. One converts money into blocks, the other converts blocks into output. Keeping them apart is what lets the same structure carry any metered service.

Historical Background

Accounting in fractions of a measure is older than the notation used for it. Scribes in Mesopotamia recorded barley rations on clay tablets in sexagesimal parts of a standard vessel, then worked out how many workers a granary could feed for how many days, which is the same conversion from a stock to a count of units of work.

Egyptian scribes handled the problem with sums of unit fractions, and the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus turns several of its exercises on dividing loaves and grain among a fixed number of recipients. In Damascus around 952, al Uqlidisi wrote on Hindu arithmetic and brought decimal fractions into written calculation, which cut the reliance on the fraction tables merchants had used for accounts. Metered cloud billing repeats the pattern with different vocabulary, since a credit is a measure and a function invocation is a ration.

Python Version

The script converts any balance into throughput using exact fractions, so it applies to any metered service where a fixed slice of the meter yields a fixed number of outputs.

from fractions import Fraction


def throughput(balance, unit_cost, output_per_unit):
    """Return the whole outputs a balance funds.

    balance         credits available
    unit_cost       credits consumed by one block of work
    output_per_unit outputs cleared by one block
    """
    blocks = Fraction(balance) / Fraction(unit_cost)
    return int(blocks) * output_per_unit


if __name__ == "__main__":
    invoices = throughput(Fraction(17, 3), Fraction(1, 3), 5)
    print(invoices)

Run it with python throughput.py and it returns 85, the invoices cleared by a balance of 5 and 2/3 credits.
