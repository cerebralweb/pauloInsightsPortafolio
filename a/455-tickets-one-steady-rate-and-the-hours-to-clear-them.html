455 TICKETS, ONE STEADY RATE, AND THE HOURS TO CLEAR THEM

In this article ❧ Support teams live and die by throughput, so a manager who cannot say when a backlog will clear cannot staff, promise, or plan against it. The bridge from a short observed window to a full clearance estimate is a single steady rate, measured once and then trusted forward. This problem shows how one measured rate converts a pile of open work into a firm hours estimate, and how Python turns that estimate into a tool the desk can reuse.

The Problem

A support team clears tickets at a constant rate. In the first 3 hours the team closes 195 tickets. A backlog of 455 tickets remains.

How many hours will the team need to clear the backlog?

  • a) 7
  • b) 7 and 1/2
  • c) 6
  • d) 9 and 1/3
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: a) 7 ✅

Math Solution

Divide 195 tickets by 3 hours to get the rate, 65 tickets per hour. Then divide the 455 backlog by that rate. The result is 7 hours.

The whole problem turns on one measured rate. 65 tickets clear each hour, so 455 tickets take 455 over 65, which is 7 hours.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Clearance rate: 195 tickets divided by 3 hours gives 65 tickets per hour
  2. Set the equation: 65 times the hours equals the 455 backlog
  3. Isolate the hours: divide 455 by 65
  4. Simplify: 455 over 65 reduces to 7
  5. Conclusion: the team needs 7 hours to clear the backlog

The Rigorous Proof

Each step is a unit. The property comes first, named, then the one inference it licenses on the numbers.

Setup. Let h be the hours needed to clear the backlog, a positive rational. The rate is 195 tickets over 3 hours. Tickets cleared is the rate times the hours. The backlog fixes tickets to clear at 455, so the rate times h equals 455.

Step 1 — Reduce the rate

Property. Cancellation law. For nonzero b, b times a over b equals a.

Inference. The rate is 65 tickets per hour. 195 over 3 is (3 times 65) over 3, and the 3 cancels, leaving 65.

Step 2 — Assemble the equation

Property. Substitution. Equals may replace equals.

Inference. The governing equation is 65 times h equals 455. Builds on line 1, replacing the rate in the relation with 65.

Step 3 — Isolate the product

Property. Multiplication property of equality. If a equals b then c times a equals c times b.

Inference. One over 65 times the quantity 65 times h equals one over 65 times 455. Builds on line 2. The same factor multiplies each side.

Step 4 — Collapse the left side

Property. Multiplicative inverse property and multiplicative identity property. A nonzero number times its inverse is one, and one times a equals a.

Inference. The left side equals h. Builds on line 3. One over 65 times 65 is one, and one times h is h.

Step 5 — Reduce the right side

Property. Cancellation law. For nonzero b, b times a over b equals a.

Inference. The right side equals 7. 455 over 65 is (65 times 7) over 65, and the 65 cancels, leaving 7.

Step 6 — Join the two sides

Property. Transitivity of equality. If a equals b and b equals c then a equals c.

Inference. Therefore h equals 7. Builds on lines 4 and 5.

Verification. 65 times 7 is 455. Tickets cleared equals the backlog.

Conclusion. The team needs 7 hours to clear the backlog.

Historical Background

Estimating a completion time from a measured pace is one of the oldest management calculations there is. Merchants and clerks formalized it as the Rule of Three, a fixed procedure for scaling one known ratio to a new quantity, and it ran through trade and workshop records long before it reached the factory floor.

Industrial time study later turned the same reasoning into a discipline, timing a sample of work to set a standard rate and then projecting output across a shift. Modern support desks and service operations use the identical move, sampling throughput over a short window and extrapolating it to a queue.

Python Version

This script turns any measured output over a window into the time needed to finish a remaining pile, so the same logic serves tickets, calls, units on a line, or invoices.

# What this does: works out how many hours are needed to clear a backlog at a measured rate.
# Where it came from: a support team closes 195 tickets in 3 hours, with 455 tickets still waiting.
# The math idea: measure a rate from a sample, then divide the backlog by that rate.
# You type in: the work done and hours in the sample, and the backlog left.
# It prints: the hours needed to clear the backlog.

# This function does the math.
def hours_to_clear(done_in_sample, sample_hours, backlog):
    # Find the rate from the sample.
    rate = done_in_sample / sample_hours
    # Divide the backlog by that rate to get the hours.
    return backlog / rate

# Tell the reader what we will figure out.
print("Let's find the hours needed to clear a backlog at a measured rate.")

# Ask for the work done in the sample.
done_in_sample = float(input("Work done in the sample (tickets): "))

# Ask for the hours in the sample.
sample_hours = float(input("Hours in the sample: "))

# Ask for the backlog left.
backlog = float(input("Backlog remaining (tickets): "))

# Do the math.
result = hours_to_clear(done_in_sample, sample_hours, backlog)

# Show a whole number when it lands on one.
result = int(result) if result.is_integer() else result

# Print the answer with words that fit the scenario.
print("Hours to clear the backlog:", result)

Run it in a terminal, enter the sample and the backlog, and it returns the hours needed. For the numbers above it prints 7.
