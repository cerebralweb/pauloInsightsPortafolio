BETTER CONVERSATIONS AND BETTER RESEARCH IN DISTRIBUTED TEAMS

In this article ❧ Three frameworks are read here as working tools for distributed teams, Duhigg on conversation types, Austin on what speech does, and Habermas on what consensus means.

Why the quality of a research product depends on the quality of the talk that surrounds it.

Two Interruptions, Before Any Chart Did Work

Two stakeholders. Two presentations. Two interruptions, arriving in the first minute, before a single chart had done any work.

The first stakeholder said: I don't want a long presentation. Tell me what to do. The second, facing a deck built on exactly that instruction, asked: why so few slides?

The data was sound in both cases. The decks were competent. Nothing had gone wrong with the research. Something had gone wrong with the conversation, and the distinction matters more than the market research profession usually admits.

We are trained to believe that a finding, once established, travels on its own merits. Get the sample right, get the model right, get the segmentation right, and the recommendation will land. This belief is comfortable, and it is false. A finding does not travel alone. It travels inside a conversation, and conversations have properties of their own — types, layers, participants with prior commitments — that determine whether anything arrives at all.

Three Types, Not One

Charles Duhigg, in Supercommunicators, argues that many failures of business communication come from a lack of awareness about which kind of conversation is actually taking place. He identifies three. The practical conversation is about decisions: what should we do. The emotional conversation is about feelings: how do we feel about this. The identity conversation is about who we are, and who we are to each other.

Think about these as three frequencies. The mismatch occurs when we are confused about which frequency we are operating in.

Return to the first story. I was presenting a report, and I believed I was in a practical conversation. I was not, or not only. Alongside the insights and the recommendations, I was also presenting methodology, assumptions, sampling logic. Why? Because I wanted the room to understand that this was rigorous work by people who know how to do it. That is not a practical move. That is an identity move — a claim about the kind of analysts we are.

The stakeholder registered the mismatch before I did. Her interruption was not a practical intervention. It was an emotional one: a signal of impatience, discomfort, a sense that her time was being spent on something other than her problem. Only then did she convert it into a practical demand. Tell me what to do.

Three steps in the sequence: practical-plus-identity, then emotional, then practical. At no point in that sequence was I matching her. The report was not the problem. The frequency was.

This is why the taxonomy earns its place. It converts a vague sense that a meeting went badly into a specific, checkable question: which conversation did they want, and which one was I having? That question can be asked in real time, which is the only time it is useful.

Three Layers in Every One of Them

The types describe what a conversation is about. A second framework describes what talking does. J. L. Austin, in How to Do Things with Words, observed that speech is not the transmission of propositions. When we talk we perform three acts at once. We say something. We mean something, which need not be what we literally said. And we produce an effect on the listener.

The third act is the one that unsettles people, because it does not belong to the speaker. I can choose my words. I can, with effort and practice, control what I mean. I cannot control what I cause. Persuasion, empathy, admiration — but also fear, and rejection. Strategies exist for making some effects more likely than others. But you cannot fully control the effects you produce.

A researcher might reasonably ask why any of this belongs in a discussion of research quality. It belongs because the effect layer is where research products succeed or fail. A slide that produces defensiveness has failed, whatever its footnotes say. A recommendation that produces the impression of being lectured has failed. Austin's point is that this failure is not a communication accident sitting outside the work.

The Context We Actually Work In

None of this happens in a vacuum. Two features of distributed research work deserve naming, because they change the difficulty of everything above.

The first is asynchrony. A team spread across time zones shares few overlapping hours, so a great deal of the communication is written. This has a genuine benefit, and it should be defended: written channels promote slow thinking. But written channels also strip the cues that carry meaning — tone, gesture, the pause before an answer. The information that tells you which of the three conversations you are in is exactly the information that written channels discard.

The second is English. In many research teams most members are not native speakers, and the working day runs in English. In a mother tongue, speaking your mind is unremarkable. In a second language it costs something, and the cost is not distributed evenly across a team. Add to this a group that merges several nationalities, several intellectual traditions, several professional backgrounds and as many accents under one work culture. English is an opportunity and a source of friction.

The honest conclusion is not that such communication is worse than anyone else's. It is that these conversations are richer and more demanding at the same time, and that the frameworks above are not optional refinements. They are compensation for missing information.

Remote Work, and What Gains Value

Remote communication removes things. Fewer gestures. Less transparency, in the era of the closed camera. Less sensory information of every kind.

The usual conclusion is that remote communication is therefore a degraded form of the real thing. A better conclusion is that the removal changes which abilities pay. When there is less information, verbal precision matters more, not less. Emotional intelligence matters more, because there is less to go on and more to infer. Experience matters more, because pattern recognition substitutes for perception.

In a remote meeting, is it easier or harder to identify which conversation the other person wants? Harder. Considerably harder. At its worst remote communication becomes an exercise in speculation.

Artificial intelligence has entered this space. Assistants in meetings help people react faster and more accurately with less information. They also raise the transparency question the closed camera already raised. Both things are true, and the practice is becoming standard whether or not we have settled our view of it.

Small Talk, Defended

Small talk is routinely dismissed as empty. As a sociologist I want to defend it, because the dismissal confuses shallow content with absent function.

Small talk performs work. It breaks the ice, which is another way of saying it establishes that the channel is open before anything is put through it. It allows a person to address a large group without addressing anyone in particular. And it permits caution: when you stumble into gossip, light conversation is the instrument that lets you participate in the room without participating in the content.

The failure is using small all the time. Teamwork over any length of time requires connections that shallow exchange cannot build, and building them requires offering something that is not obvious about yourself — a small piece of vulnerability, at the right moment, with respect. Not a confession. A disclosure proportionate to the relationship.

Synchrony, and Why It Is Not Manipulation

Duhigg draws on research into group communication to describe participants of high centrality: the people who, without dominating, hold groups together. They match their partner's mode — seriousness with seriousness, emotion with emotion. They speak less, and mostly to ask questions. They repeat other people's ideas and use them. They admit mistakes. They hold larger networks than average. They are rarely the cleverest person present, and when they speak, everyone listens.

What they produce is synchrony: the degree of consensual dependency in a group. Both words carry weight.

Dependency first, because we have been overwhelmed for decades by the claim that dependency is a defect and independence a virtue. For a social animal this is a strange position. Consensual dependency is what a strong team is made of, and it is the condition of cooperation over time.

Then consensual. Here Jürgen Habermas is useful. His distinction is between communicative action, oriented to mutual understanding, and strategic action, oriented to advantage. Consensus in the first sense is not compliance. It is agreement reached because the reasons persuaded, not because the power relations settled the matter.

This answers the objection that matching your interlocutor is a form of manipulation. Matching in the service of advantage is manipulation. Matching in the service of understanding is not, and the difference is legible to everyone in the room, which is why high-centrality participants are described as authentic rather than skilled. Synchrony is cooperation, and cooperation is not competition conducted politely.

The Return on All of This

Why should organizations care? Because synchrony makes disagreement safe, and disagreement is a quality-control mechanism. In a group with high competition and low cooperation, dissent is expensive, so people withhold it. Weak findings survive. In a group with synchrony, weak findings get challenged early, which is the only cheap moment to challenge them.

I want to bring the argument back to the first story. Once I spotted the mismatch, I could reframe. The stakeholder understood that the presentation was not a threat to her agenda. She relaxed, and the work finished. The conversation ended up on toddlers, because she was a parent and so was I — an identity neither side had thought to bring into the room.

Every person holds several identities at once. Finding the one you share is not a trick. It is the ordinary means by which a conversation becomes a negotiation, quietly, in real time. Being flexible and observant is what makes a difficult conversation solvable.
