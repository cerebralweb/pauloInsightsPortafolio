FIVE CLOUD ACCOUNTS, TWELVE MONTHS, AND THE RENEWALS THAT COLLIDE

In this article ❧ Cloud commitments renew on a calendar, and finance teams plan cash and review work around those dates. Two renewals in the same month concentrate payments and approvals, so the chance of a shared month shapes how a team groups its accounts. This article builds that chance one account at a time, shows each step in an interactive graph, proves the result with a named property for each step, and turns it into a reusable Python tool.

The Problem

A FinOps team manages the 35 cloud accounts of Region 9. The team splits the accounts into groups of 5 to plan commitment renewals across the fiscal year.

The team asks what would be the best way to estimate the probability that in each group at least 2 accounts renew in the same month.

  • a) flipping a coin 35 times to record whether each account renews in an even or an odd month
  • b) using a random number generator with the range 01 to 05, one number for each possible account
  • c) putting 5 cards in a bag, one for each account in a group, and drawing one card for each account that matches another account
  • d) dividing a spinner into 12 equal sectors, one for each month, and spinning it 5 times for each trial the team wants to record
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: d) A spinner with 12 equal sectors, spun 5 times per trial ✅

Math Solution

Each spin gives one account a month with a chance of 1/12, so 5 spins model one group of 5 accounts. Counting the ways all 5 months differ, 12 × 11 × 10 × 9 × 8 = 95,040 out of 12⁵ = 248,832, and taking the opposite event gives the value the spinner trials approach, 89/144 ≈ 61.8%.

The opposite event, all months different, stays one case at any group size, while a shared month splits into more patterns with each account added.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Model choice: The spinner gives 12 equal outcomes, one per month. Option a gives 2 outcomes, option b draws accounts, and option c has no months.
  2. One trial: 5 spins, one per account in the group.
  3. Sample space: 12⁵ = 248,832 outcomes for a group of 5.
  4. All months different: 12 × 11 × 10 × 9 × 8 = 95,040 outcomes.
  5. Chance all months differ: 95,040/248,832 = 55/144 ≈ 38.2%.
  6. Chance of a shared month: 1 − 55/144 = 89/144 ≈ 61.8%.

The Scaffolding, Step by Step

A FinOps team splits 35 cloud accounts into groups of 5. Each account renews its cloud commitment once a year, in one of 12 months, and each month has the same chance. Two renewals in one month concentrate payments and review work in that month. The team wants the chance that at least 2 accounts in a group renew in the same month.

This section builds the answer from one account to five, one problem at a time.

Method

Three questions come first.

  1. What are the possible outcomes? Their list is the sample space.
  2. Which outcomes does the question ask about? That set is the event.
  3. Which outcomes remain? That set is the opposite event. An event and its opposite event add to 1.

Definitions

  • Outcome. One possible result, e.g. account A renews in March.
  • Sample space. The set of all possible outcomes, written S. For one account, S holds 12 months.
  • Event. A set of outcomes that the question asks about, written E, e.g. E = {March}.
  • Opposite event. The outcomes in S that are not in E, written "not E." Its other name is the complement.
  • Probability. The number of outcomes in E divided by the number of outcomes in S, when each outcome has the same chance. Written P(E).
  • Complement rule. P(E) + P(not E) = 1. It reads "the probability of E plus the probability of not E equals 1," e.g. 1/12 + 11/12 = 1.
  • Parameter. A value the method takes as input and that changes the result, e.g. the number of accounts.
  • Independent events. Events where one outcome does not change the chance of the other. When independent events must both happen, you multiply their chances.
  • Mutually exclusive events. Events that cannot happen together. To find the chance that one of them happens, you add their chances.

One account

Step 1. One account has 12 possible outcomes, one for each month.

Step 2. The first question is what the possible outcomes are. Their list is the sample space. For one account, the sample space holds 12 months. One of them is March.

1/12 ≈ 0.083 ≈ 8.3%

Step 3. Eleven of the 12 outcomes are months other than March.

11/12 ≈ 0.917 ≈ 91.7%

1/12 + 11/12 = 1. An event and its opposite event add to 1, since one of the two always happens.

Two accounts

Step 4. For two accounts, the sample space holds 144 outcomes.

12 × 12 = 144

When one choice has 12 options and a second choice has 12, the pair has 12 × 12 outcomes. This is the multiplication rule for counting.

Step 5. One outcome has both A and B in March.

1/144 ≈ 0.007 ≈ 0.7%

A second route gives the same number.

1/12 × 1/12 = 1/144

For n accounts, the chance that all renew in March is (1/12)ⁿ. Each account added multiplies the chance by 1/12.

Step 6. Twelve outcomes have A and B in the same month, one for each month.

12/144 = 1/12 ≈ 8.3%

A second route. Each month case has a chance of 1/144. The 12 cases cannot happen together, since A renews in one month. So their chances add.

12 × 1/144 = 1/12

Step 7. "Different months" is the opposite event of "same month."

1 − 12/144 = 132/144 = 11/12 ≈ 91.7%

Counting gives the same result. 144 − 12 = 132 outcomes with different months.

A third route. A takes any month. B takes one of the 11 months A did not take.

12/12 × 11/12 = 11/12

Three accounts

Step 8. For three accounts, the sample space holds 1,728 outcomes.

12³ = 12 × 12 × 12 = 1,728

Step 9. Twelve outcomes have A, B, and C in the same month, one for each month.

12/12³ = 1/12² = 1/144 ≈ 0.7%

A second route. A takes any month. B must match A, and C must match A.

1 × 1/12 × 1/12 = 1/144

Step 10. Three pairs of accounts can share a month. A with B, A with C, and B with C.

For the pair A with B, 132 outcomes have A and B in one month and C in another.

132/1,728 ≈ 7.6%

Three pairs, 132 outcomes each.

3 × 132/1,728 = 396/1,728 ≈ 22.9%

Step 11. For 3 accounts, the chance that at least 2 share a month is 408/1,728 ≈ 23.6%. The two cases are mutually exclusive, so their chances add.

(12 + 396)/1,728 = 408/1,728 ≈ 23.6%

Step 12. For 3 accounts, the chance that all 3 renew in different months is 1,320/1,728 ≈ 76.4%. "All different" is the opposite event of "at least 2 share," so their chances add to 1.

408/1,728 + 1,320/1,728 = 1,728/1,728 = 1

A second count, one account at a time. A has 12 months, B has 11, and C has 10.

12 × 11 × 10 = 1,320

This count needs no cases. The case count for "at least 2 share" grows with each account. This product grows by one factor.

Five accounts

Step 13. For 5 accounts, 95,040 outcomes have all 5 in different months. Each account excludes the months the earlier accounts took.

12 × 11 × 10 × 9 × 8 = 95,040

The chance that all 5 renew in different months is 95,040/248,832 ≈ 38.2%.

The chance that at least 2 of the 5 accounts renew in the same month is 1 − 0.382 = 0.618 ≈ 61.8%.

Model Assumptions

Each account renews independent of the others. Each month has the same chance. Real renewals can cluster, e.g. at fiscal year end. Unequal chances raise the chance of a shared month above the model's result.

Concepts and Tools Used

The scaffolding uses ratios, not rates. A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind, e.g. 12 outcomes to 144 outcomes. Each probability here is a ratio of outcomes. A rate compares quantities with different units, e.g. renewals per month.

Fraction properties used.

  • Reducing, e.g. 12/144 = 1/12 and 132/144 = 11/12.
  • Multiplying fractions, e.g. 1/12 × 1/12 = 1/144.
  • Multiplying a fraction by a whole number, e.g. 12 × 1/144 = 1/12.
  • Adding fractions with a common denominator, e.g. (12 + 396)/1,728.
  • Subtracting from 1, with 1 written as 12/12 or 1,728/1,728.
  • Converting fractions to decimals and percents.

Other tools used.

  • Powers, e.g. 12³, and the power of a fraction, (1/12)ⁿ = 1/12ⁿ.
  • The multiplication rule for counting.
  • Order of operations, e.g. the parentheses in step 11.
  • Two counting ideas used without their names. The 3 pairs from A, B, and C are combinations, selections where order does not matter. The product 12 × 11 × 10 × 9 × 8 counts permutations, arrangements where order matters and no month repeats.

    Formal Solution

    Every step below names the property that licenses it, states the property in general form, and shows it acting on the numbers.

    Mode

    Compute.

    Setup

    A group holds 5 accounts. Each account renews in one of 12 months. Each month has the same chance for each account, and the accounts renew independent of one another. An outcome lists the renewal month of each account in order. M is the event that at least 2 accounts renew in the same month. P(M) is the unknown. The route counts the complement of M, then subtracts its probability from 1.

    Notation

    • S is the sample space, every ordered list of 5 months with one month per account.
    • ω is one outcome in S, one such list.
    • |X| is the number of outcomes in a set X.
    • M is the event that at least 2 of the 5 accounts renew in the same month.
    • Mᶜ is the complement of M, the event that the 5 accounts renew in 5 different months.
    • P(X) is the probability of an event X. P(M) is the unknown.
    • k is the position of an account in the list, from 1 to 5.
    • 12 is the number of months, and 5 is the number of accounts in a group.
    • x, y, z, w are any numbers, and X, Y are any events, used to state a property in general form.
    • r is a number of successive choices, and c₁, …, cᵣ are the options at each choice, used in general form.
    • xⁿ is x multiplied by itself n times, n a positive integer.
    • X ∩ Y is the event that X and Y both happen.
    • ⇒ reads implies.
    • ≠ reads is not equal to.
    • ≈ reads is about equal to.

    Step 1. Multiplication Rule for Counting

    Property. Multiplication rule for counting. r successive choices with c₁, …, cᵣ options give (c₁)(c₂)…(cᵣ) outcomes.

    Inference. |S| = (12)(12)(12)(12)(12).

    Step 2. Definition of a Power

    Property. Definition of a power. xⁿ = (x)(x)…(x), with n factors.

    Inference. |S| = 12⁵ = 248,832. From line 1.

    Step 3. Multiplication Rule for Independent Events

    Property. Multiplication rule for independent events. P(X ∩ Y) = P(X)P(Y).

    Inference. P(ω) = (1/12)(1/12)(1/12)(1/12)(1/12) = (1/12)⁵.

    Step 4. Power of a Quotient

    Property. Power of a quotient. (x/y)ⁿ = xⁿ/yⁿ, for y ≠ 0.

    Inference. P(ω) = 1⁵/12⁵ = 1/248,832, the same for every ω. From lines 2 and 3.

    Step 5. Multiplication Rule for Counting, Again

    Property. Multiplication rule for counting, as in Step 1.

    Inference. Account k avoids the k − 1 months taken before it, so |Mᶜ| = (12)(11)(10)(9)(8).

    Step 6. Associativity of Multiplication

    Property. Associativity of multiplication. (xy)z = x(yz).

    Inference. |Mᶜ| = ((12)(11))((10)(9))(8) = (132)(90)(8) = 95,040. From line 5.

    Step 7. Classical Definition of Probability

    Property. Classical definition of probability. When every outcome has the same chance, P(X) = |X|/|S|.

    Inference. P(Mᶜ) = 95,040/248,832. From lines 2, 4, and 6.

    Step 8. Cancellation Law

    Property. Cancellation law. (yx)/(yz) = x/z, for y ≠ 0 and z ≠ 0.

    Inference. P(Mᶜ) = (1,728)(55)/((1,728)(144)) = 55/144. From line 7.

    Step 9. Complement Rule

    Property. Complement rule. P(X) + P(Xᶜ) = 1.

    Inference. P(M) + 55/144 = 1. From line 8.

    Step 10. Subtraction Property of Equality

    Property. Subtraction property of equality. x = y ⇒ x − z = y − z.

    Inference. P(M) = 1 − 55/144. From line 9.

    Step 11. Multiplicative Inverse

    Property. Multiplicative inverse. y(1/y) = y/y = 1, for y ≠ 0.

    Inference. P(M) = 144/144 − 55/144. From line 10.

    Step 12. Distributive Law

    Property. Distributive law. x(1/z) − y(1/z) = (x − y)(1/z).

    Inference. P(M) = (144 − 55)/144 = 89/144. From line 11.

    Step 13. Division Algorithm

    Property. Division algorithm. For integers x ≥ 0 and y > 0, x = qy + w with integers q and 0 ≤ w < y.

    Inference. 890,000 = (6,180)(144) + 80 ⇒ 89/144 = 0.6180 + 80/1,440,000 ≈ 0.618. From line 12.

    Verification

    P(M) + P(Mᶜ) = 89/144 + 55/144 = 144/144 = 1.

    |M| = 248,832 − 95,040 = 153,792 = (1,728)(89), and 153,792/248,832 = 89/144.

    Answer

    P(M) = 89/144 ≈ 0.618. In a group of 5 accounts, the chance that at least 2 renew in the same month is about 61.8%.

    Historical Background

    The question of shared dates is known as the birthday problem, and Richard von Mises published the first version of it in 1939. In the late 1940s, Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann used repeated random trials at Los Alamos to estimate outcomes too complex to count, a method that took the name Monte Carlo. The same logic sizes collision risk in scheduling, payment calendars, and data systems.

    Python Version

    The script returns the exact chance of a shared renewal month for any group size and any number of months, then checks it with a spinner test, so the same file serves the next portfolio.

    # What this does. Works out the exact chance that at least 2 accounts in a group renew in the same month, then checks it with a spinner test.
# Where it came from. A FinOps team split 35 cloud accounts into groups of 5, and each account renews in one of 12 months.
# The math idea. Count the ways every account gets its own month, divide by all the ways, take that from 1, then compare with spins.
# You type in. How many accounts are in a group, how many months an account can renew in, and how many spinner tests to run.
# It prints. The exact chance as a fraction and a percent, and the share of spinner tests with a shared month.

import math
import random
from fractions import Fraction

# This function finds the exact chance.
def exact_chance(accounts, months):
    # Count the ways every account gets its own month, e.g. 12 x 11 x 10 x 9 x 8.
    all_different_ways = math.perm(months, accounts)
    # Count every way the accounts can pick months, e.g. 12 to the power 5.
    all_ways = months ** accounts
    # Keep the chance as a fraction, then take it away from 1.
    return 1 - Fraction(all_different_ways, all_ways)

# This function spins a wheel with one slot per month, many times.
def spinner_test(accounts, months, tests):
    # Count the tests where 2 or more accounts land on the same month.
    shared = 0
    for _ in range(tests):
        # Spin once for each account.
        spins = [random.randrange(months) for _ in range(accounts)]
        # If a month came up twice, the list of different months is shorter.
        if len(set(spins)) < accounts:
            shared = shared + 1
    # The share of tests with a shared month.
    return shared / tests

# This function asks the questions and prints the answers.
def run():
    # Tell the reader what we will figure out.
    print("Let's find the chance that 2 renewals fall in the same month.")
    # Ask for the number of accounts in one group.
    accounts = int(input("How many accounts are in a group? "))
    # Ask for the number of months an account can renew in.
    months = int(input("How many months can an account renew in? "))
    # Ask how many spinner tests to run.
    tests = int(input("How many spinner tests should we run? "))
    # Run at least 1 test, so the share makes sense.
    tests = max(tests, 1)
    # Do the exact math.
    exact = exact_chance(accounts, months)
    # Run the spinner tests.
    estimate = spinner_test(accounts, months, tests)
    # Print the exact answer as a fraction and a percent.
    print(f"Exact chance that at least 2 of {accounts} accounts share a renewal month is {exact}, about {round(float(exact) * 100, 1)}%.")
    # Print what the spinner tests found.
    print(f"Spinner tests with a shared month came to {round(estimate * 100, 1)}% of {tests} tests.")

# Start the program.
run()

    Paste the script into Python in Windows Terminal, enter 5 accounts, 12 months, and a number of tests, and it returns 89/144, about 61.8%, beside the spinner estimate.
