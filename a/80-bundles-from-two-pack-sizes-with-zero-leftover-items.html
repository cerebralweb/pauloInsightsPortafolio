80 BUNDLES FROM TWO PACK SIZES WITH ZERO LEFTOVER ITEMS

In this article ❧ When two supplies arrive in fixed pack sizes and neither can be split, the build count is not a free choice. A team that picks the wrong number ends up holding partial packs it paid for but cannot use. This problem shows how the least common multiple sets the smallest production run that consumes whole packs of every component at once, so procurement waste stays at zero.

The Problem

Your operations team assembles new hire welcome bundles. Each bundle needs one laptop sleeve and one keycard. Sleeves ship in boxes of 20. Keycards ship in packs of 16.

How many bundles should you build, and how many boxes of sleeves and packs of keycards should you order so that nothing is left over?

  • a) 80 bundles, 4 boxes of sleeves, and 5 packs of keycards
  • b) 40 bundles, 4 boxes of sleeves, and 5 packs of keycards
  • c) 80 bundles, 8 boxes of sleeves, and 5 packs of keycards
  • d) 20 bundles, 4 boxes of sleeves, and 10 packs of keycards
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: a) 80 bundles, 4 boxes of sleeves, and 5 packs of keycards ✅

Math Solution

Find the least common multiple of the two pack sizes. Factor 20 as 2² times 5 and 16 as 2⁴, take the highest power of each prime, and multiply to get 80. Then divide 80 by each pack size to size the order.

The smallest run that empties whole packs of both supplies is where their multiples first meet, which is 80.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Factor each pack size: 20 = 2² × 5, and 16 = 2⁴
  2. Highest power of each prime: 2⁴ and 5
  3. Least common multiple: 2⁴ × 5 = 80 bundles
  4. Boxes of sleeves: 80 ÷ 20 = 4, remainder 0
  5. Packs of keycards: 80 ÷ 16 = 5, remainder 0
  6. Result: 80 bundles, 4 boxes, 5 packs, nothing left over

Proof Version

The same answer, written so each step names the property that licenses it. Mode is compute.

Setup. Let N be the smallest number of items that empties whole boxes of sleeves and whole packs of keycards at once, a positive integer. Sleeves ship 20 per box and keycards ship 16 per pack, so N must be a common multiple of 20 and 16, and the smallest such N is their least common multiple. Then boxes of sleeves equal N over 20 and packs of keycards equal N over 16.

  1. Fundamental theorem of arithmetic. Every integer greater than one has a unique factorization into primes. So 20 factors as 2² times 5, and 16 factors as 2⁴.
  2. Greatest common divisor by shared prime powers. The GCD is the product of each shared prime raised to its lowest power present in both. The only shared prime is 2, at lowest power 2², so the GCD of 20 and 16 is 4. Builds on step 1.
  3. Product identity for LCM and GCD. For positive integers a and b, LCM times GCD equals a times b, so LCM equals a times b over GCD. Here 20 times 16 is 320, divided by 4, giving an LCM of 80. Builds on step 2.
  4. Division algorithm. For integers n and d greater than zero, there exist unique q and r with n equal to d times q plus r and r between 0 and d. Here 80 equals 20 times 4 plus 0, so 4 boxes of sleeves and remainder 0. Builds on step 3.
  5. Division algorithm, as in step 4. 80 equals 16 times 5 plus 0, so 5 packs of keycards and remainder 0. Builds on step 3.

Verification. 4 boxes times 20 is 80 sleeves. 5 packs times 16 is 80 keycards. Both equal 80, so both remainders are 0 and nothing is left over.

Answer. Build 80 bundles, order 4 boxes of sleeves and 5 packs of keycards. Option a.

Historical Background

The instinct behind this calculation is old. Ancient calendar keepers faced the same puzzle when they tried to line up cycles of different lengths, such as lunar months against solar years, and asked when two separate counts would fall on the same day again. A Chinese arithmetic manual attributed to Sunzi, from roughly the third to fifth century, posed counting puzzles of exactly this shape and seeded the ideas that later became the Chinese Remainder Theorem. Modern procurement inherits the same question whenever parts arrive in set lot sizes and a plan has to bring them into balance without waste.

Python Version

This short script sizes a run for any two pack sizes, so it stays useful well beyond sleeves and keycards.

from math import gcd

def plan_run(pack_a, pack_b):
    run = pack_a * pack_b // gcd(pack_a, pack_b)
    return run, run // pack_a, run // pack_b

bundles, boxes_a, packs_b = plan_run(20, 16)
print(bundles, boxes_a, packs_b)

Run it with the two pack sizes and it returns the bundle count and the order for each supply, here 80, 4, and 5.
