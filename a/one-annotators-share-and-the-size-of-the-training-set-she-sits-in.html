ONE ANNOTATOR'S SHARE, AND THE SIZE OF THE TRAINING SET SHE SITS IN

In this article ❧ Teams that build training data rarely count the whole set directly. They count what one contributor delivered, know what share of the work that contributor owns, and infer the rest, which is how a project manager sizes a dataset before it exists. This problem shows how one known share fixes an unknown total, and how four routes to that total rest on four different named properties.

The Problem

Angela labeled 24 images for the training set her team will contribute to a computer vision project. Her 24 images are 15% of the total her team will contribute. The platform vendor then donated 50 labeled images.

How many labeled images are there in total?

  • a) 80
  • b) 160
  • c) 210
  • d) 290
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: c) 210 ✅

Math Solution

The share statement gives the equation (15/100)t = 24, so the team total follows from dividing 24 by 15%, which yields t = 160. The vendor donation is added after the total is known, so G = 160 + 50 = 210.

Fifteen percent of the work carries the whole set, since 24 images per 15 points of share means 1.6 images per point, and 100 points of share means 160 images.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Share as an equation: (15/100)t = 24
  2. One point of share: 24/15 = 1.6 images
  3. Team total: (1.6)(100) = 160 images
  4. Vendor donation: 50 images, added after the total
  5. Grand total: G = 160 + 50 = 210 images

Setup and Notation

All four proofs share one setup and one notation list, so they differ only in the property that carries the work.

Let t be the team total and G the grand total. Then (15/100)t = 24 and G = t + 50.

  • t is the team total in labeled images, the unknown of stage one
  • G is the grand total in labeled images, the unknown asked for
  • 24 is the images Angela labeled, 50 the images the vendor donated
  • 15/100 is fifteen percent as a fraction, 0.15 the same number as a decimal
  • 3/20 is 15/100 in lowest terms
  • 100/15 is the multiplicative inverse of 15/100
  • gcd(x, y) is the largest integer dividing both x and y
  • ⇒ reads implies

Four Proofs

Each step below names the property that licenses it, states that property in general form, then shows it acting on these numbers. Every step after the first cites the line it rests on.

Route A, scale the share

  1. Definition of percent. p% of x = (p/100)x.
    Inference. (15/100)t = 24.
  2. Division property of equality. x = y and c ≠ 0 ⇒ x/c = y/c.
    Inference. (5/100)t = 8, taking c = 3, since gcd(15, 24) = 3. From A1.
  3. Multiplication property of equality. x = y ⇒ cx = cy.
    Inference. (20)((5/100)t) = (20)(8) = 160. From A2.
  4. Multiplicative identity. 1x = x, and 100/100 = 1.
    Inference. t = 160. From A3.
  5. Substitution. x = y ⇒ x may replace y.
    Inference. G = 160 + 50 = 210. From A4.

Route B, one point of share

  1. Definition of percent. As in A1.
    Inference. (15/100)t = 24.
  2. Division property of equality. As in A2.
    Inference. (1/100)t = 24/15 = 1.6, taking c = 15. From B1.
  3. Multiplication property of equality. As in A3.
    Inference. t = (100)(1.6) = 160. From B2.
  4. Substitution. As in A5.
    Inference. G = 160 + 50 = 210. From B3.

Route C, decimal equation

  1. Definition of percent. As in A1.
    Inference. (0.15)t = 24.
  2. Division property of equality. As in A2.
    Inference. t = 24/0.15. From C1.
  3. Fundamental property of fractions. For c ≠ 0, x/y = (cx)/(cy).
    Inference. 24/0.15 = 2400/15, taking c = 100. From C2.
  4. Cancellation law. For y ≠ 0, (yx)/y = x.
    Inference. 2400/15 = (15)(160)/15 = 160. From C3.
  5. Transitivity of equality. x = y and y = z ⇒ x = z.
    Inference. t = 160. From C2, C3, C4.
  6. Substitution. As in A5.
    Inference. G = 160 + 50 = 210. From C5.

Route D, multiplicative inverse

  1. Definition of percent. As in A1.
    Inference. 24 = (15/100)t.
  2. Multiplication property of equality. As in A3.
    Inference. (100/15)(24) = (100/15)((15/100)t). From D1.
  3. Multiplicative inverse and multiplicative identity. For x ≠ 0, (1/x)x = 1 and 1x = x.
    Inference. (100/15)((15/100)t) = (1)t = t. From D2.
  4. Cancellation law. As in C4.
    Inference. (100/15)(24) = 2400/15 = (15)(160)/15 = 160.
  5. Transitivity of equality. As in C5.
    Inference. t = 160. From D3 and D4.
  6. Substitution. As in A5.
    Inference. G = 160 + 50 = 210. From D5.

Shared verification

(15/100)(160) = (3/20)(160) = 24, and 160 + 50 = 210. Both constraints hold, so all four routes land on G = 210.

Reading the Comparison

Two properties appear in all four routes. The definition of percent turns the statement into an equation, and substitution turns the team total into the grand total. Neither distinguishes anything, since they are the spine every route stands on.

Three properties appear in exactly one route. The multiplicative inverse belongs to Route D alone, the fundamental property of fractions to Route C alone. Each is the signature of its route.

Route A and Route B are the same proof at different scale factors. Both pair the division property with the multiplication property, A dividing by 3 and multiplying by 20, B dividing by 15 and multiplying by 100. Route A pays one extra property for the shortcut, the multiplicative identity that collapses 100/100 to 1.

Route C and Route D converge on the same figure, 2400/15, and both close with the cancellation law and transitivity. They differ on how the divisor moves. Route C divides, then rewrites the fraction. Route D multiplies by the inverse, which is why it never needs the division property at all.

Which Route to Use

Route A when the share and the count share a factor, as 15 and 24 do here. The shortcut depends on that accident, so it fails on the next dataset.

Route B for ordinary work. It is the shortest honest path, it needs no shared factor, and one point of share is the figure a spreadsheet wants anyway.

Route D for a documented calculation. Six named properties per five lines is the highest justification density of the four, and the multiplicative inverse states why the equation may be divided at all, which is the question an auditor asks.

Historical Background

Working back from a part to a whole is older than the percent sign. Roman tax and interest practice reckoned in hundredths, centesima rerum venalium on sales and centesima usura on loans, so an official who knew a levy and its rate could recover the taxable base. The device that made the calculation routine came from medieval Islamic scholarship, where al-Khwarizmi set out the operations of restoring and balancing an equation, the moves that let an unknown total be isolated rather than guessed.

The commercial version spread through the Italian abbacus schools of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, whose merchant students drilled the rule of three on exactly this shape of problem, one known part against its share, solving for the whole. Percent notation followed from the printing houses of Renaissance Europe, and the practice moved intact into modern operations, where a project manager sizes a dataset from one annotator's output the same way a Florentine factor sized a cargo from one partner's stake.
