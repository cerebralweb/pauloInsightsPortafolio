2,277 MODELS IN PRODUCTION, AND THE BASELINE A CLOUD TEAM FORGOT

In this article ❧ Operating metrics rarely arrive with their own history attached. A platform team knows what it runs today and knows how each year compared with the one before, yet the starting point of the trend sits two steps back and has to be recovered. This problem shows how a chain of year over year comparisons can be reversed to recover a baseline, and why every step in that reversal rests on a property that can be named.

The Problem

A cloud platform team tracks how many GenAI models it deployed to production each year. In 2018 it deployed 2,277 models, which was 110% of the models deployed in 2017. The 2017 figure was 90% of the models deployed in 2016.

How many models did the team deploy in 2016?

  • a) 2,277
  • b) 2,077
  • c) 2,070
  • d) 2,300
Click to Reveal the Answer

Correct Answer: d) 2,300 ✅

Math Solution

Each year is a percentage of the year before it, so the chain is walked backwards by dividing rather than multiplying. Divide 2,277 by 1.1 to recover 2017, then divide that result by 0.9 to recover 2016.

A 10% rise on top of a 10% fall does not return to the starting point, which is why 2016 stands above 2018.

Step-by-Step Breakdown

The following sequence shows how the final amount is derived:

  1. Known year: 2018 deployments are 2,277
  2. Relationship for 2018: 2,277 = 1.1 × (2017 deployments)
  3. Reverse it: 2,277 ÷ 1.1 = 22,770 ÷ 11 = 2,070 models in 2017
  4. Relationship for 2017: 2,070 = 0.9 × (2016 deployments)
  5. Reverse it: 2,070 ÷ 0.9 = 20,700 ÷ 9 = 2,300 models in 2016
  6. Check: 0.9 × 2,300 = 2,070 and 1.1 × 2,070 = 2,277
  7. Conclusion: the team deployed 2,300 models in 2016, more than in either of the two years that followed

The Solution With Every Property Named

The breakdown above gives the arithmetic. What follows gives the licence for each move, one property per step.

Setup

Let n16, n17, and n18 be the numbers of models deployed in 2016, 2017, and 2018. The 2018 count is fixed at 2,277. The 2018 count equals 110 percent of the 2017 count, so n18 = (11/10)n17. The 2017 count equals 90 percent of the 2016 count, so n17 = (9/10)n16. The unknown is n16.

Notation

  • n16 is the count of models deployed in 2016, the unknown, a positive integer.
  • n17 is the count of models deployed in 2017, 2070 once Step 5 fixes it.
  • n18 is the count of models deployed in 2018, 2277.
  • 11/10 is 110 percent written as a fraction.
  • 9/10 is 90 percent written as a fraction.
  • 10/11 is the multiplicative inverse of 11/10.
  • 10/9 is the multiplicative inverse of 9/10.
  • x, y, c are any real numbers, used to state a property in general form.
  • ⇒ reads implies.
  • ≠ reads is not equal to.

Step 1, Substitution

Property. Substitution. x = y ⇒ x may replace y.

Inference. n18 = 2277 and n18 = (11/10)n17, so (11/10)n17 = 2277.

Step 2, Multiplication property of equality

Property. Multiplication property of equality. x = y ⇒ cx = cy.

Inference. (10/11)((11/10)n17) = (10/11)(2277). From line 1.

Step 3, Multiplicative inverse and multiplicative identity

Property. Multiplicative inverse and multiplicative identity. For x ≠ 0, (1/x)x = 1 and 1x = x.

Inference. (10/11)((11/10)n17) = (1)n17 = n17. From line 2.

Step 4, Cancellation law

Property. Cancellation law. For y ≠ 0, (yx)/y = x.

Inference. (10/11)(2277) = 22770/11 = (11)(2070)/11 = 2070.

Step 5, Transitivity of equality

Property. Transitivity of equality. x = y and y = z ⇒ x = z.

Inference. n17 = 2070. From lines 3 and 4.

Step 6, Substitution

Property. Substitution, as in Step 1.

Inference. n17 = (9/10)n16 and n17 = 2070, so (9/10)n16 = 2070. From line 5.

Step 7, Multiplication property of equality

Property. Multiplication property of equality, as in Step 2.

Inference. (10/9)((9/10)n16) = (10/9)(2070). From line 6.

Step 8, Multiplicative inverse and multiplicative identity

Property. Multiplicative inverse and multiplicative identity, as in Step 3.

Inference. (10/9)((9/10)n16) = (1)n16 = n16. From line 7.

Step 9, Cancellation law

Property. Cancellation law, as in Step 4.

Inference. (10/9)(2070) = 20700/9 = (9)(2300)/9 = 2300.

Step 10, Transitivity of equality

Property. Transitivity of equality, as in Step 5.

Inference. n16 = 2300. From lines 8 and 9.

Verification

(9/10)(2300) = 2070 and (11/10)(2070) = 2277. Both constraints match.

Answer

n16 = 2300. In 2016 the team deployed 2,300 models, which is option d.

Clearing a Decimal Divisor

Written with decimals, the first reversal reads 2277/9.9 rather than 2277/(11/10). Multiplying divisor and dividend by 10 clears the decimal and turns the division into 22770/99, which reduces to 230. Three properties license that move.

The rule on top is the fundamental property of fractions, the cancellation law read in reverse. For c ≠ 0 and y ≠ 0, x/y = (cx)/(cy). Applied here, 2277/9.9 = ((10)(2277))/((10)(9.9)) = 22770/99 = 230.

Underneath sit the definition of division, x/y = x(1/y) for y ≠ 0, the inverse of a product, 1/(cy) = (1/c)(1/y) for c ≠ 0 and y ≠ 0, and the pair of multiplicative inverse and multiplicative identity, c(1/c) = 1 and 1x = x. With commutativity and associativity of multiplication the chain closes.

(cx)/(cy) = (cx)(1/(cy)) = (cx)((1/c)(1/y)) = (c(1/c))(x(1/y)) = (1)(x/y) = x/y.

The scaling works because 10 ≠ 0. With c = 0 both sides collapse to 0/0 and the equality fails.

The Inverse of a Product

The rule states that for x ≠ 0 and y ≠ 0, 1/(xy) = (1/x)(1/y). Dividing by a product equals dividing by one factor and then by the other. In group notation the same rule reads (xy)⁻¹ = y⁻¹x⁻¹, with the order reversed. Order matters when multiplication is not commutative, as with matrices. For numbers the order is free.

Two consequences carry most of the use. Division becomes multiplication by a reciprocal, since x/y = x(1/y). And a reciprocal splits across factors, so a division by many factors can be taken one factor at a time.

History

Reciprocals came before the rule. Scribes in Mesopotamia around 1800 BCE kept reciprocal tables in base 60 and divided by looking up 1/y and multiplying. Tablets from Nippur pair regular numbers with their reciprocals for that purpose. Egyptian practice in the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, around 1550 BCE, ran on unit fractions and treated division as a search for parts.

The rule itself became explicit once multiplication and inverses were stated as axioms. Cayley set out group axioms in 1854, and the reversal law (xy)⁻¹ = y⁻¹x⁻¹ belongs to that account. Weber and Dedekind gave the field axioms their modern form in the 1890s, where the inverse of a product follows from associativity, commutativity, and the existence of a multiplicative inverse. Matrix algebra, worked out by Cayley and Sylvester in the same period, supplied the case where order cannot be dropped.

Use in Business

  • Rate conversion. A figure per year divided by 12 months and 5 regions equals the figure times (1/12)(1/5). Each divisor can be applied on its own, which is why per unit metrics can be built in stages.
  • Currency chains. Converting A to B to C multiplies two rates. Reversing the chain multiplies the two reciprocals.
  • Discounting. The factor 1/(1+r)ⁿ is a product of n reciprocals. A cash flow can be discounted period by period or in one operation, with the same result.
  • Matrix work. Regression, portfolio optimization, and Leontief input output models invert products of matrices. Here the reversal matters, since (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹ and not A⁻¹B⁻¹.
  • Decimal cleanup. Scaling a divisor and a dividend by 10 rests on the same rule, since 1/(10y) = (1/10)(1/y).

Historical Background

Working backwards from a known total to an unknown starting amount is one of the oldest recorded uses of commercial arithmetic. Scribes in Mesopotamia kept tables of reciprocals so that a division could be carried out as a multiplication, which let them settle loans, rations, and grain shares from a final quantity rather than a starting one.

Medieval merchants met the same task under the name regula falsi, a guess and correct routine taught for problems where the opening capital had to be recovered from the closing balance. Italian abacus schools of the fourteenth century drilled it on profit shares and currency chains, and Fibonacci carried it into Europe in the Liber Abaci.

The modern version lives in index rebasing and in growth reporting. Statistical offices and finance teams publish each period as a percentage of the one before it, which means any baseline has to be reconstructed by reversing the chain. The lesson merchants learned early still holds, since a fall followed by an equal rise does not restore the original figure.
